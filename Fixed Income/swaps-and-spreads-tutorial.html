A swap is just one set of payments exchanged for another. From that single idea we'll build par pricing, carry, the swap curve as a benchmark, and finish on the deep stuff: what actually drives swap spreads, and why a long-end flattener is secretly an option.

SECTION 01

What a swap actually is

Strip away the jargon and a swap is one promise: I'll pay you this stream, you pay me that stream. Get the vocabulary nailed here and the rest of the deck unlocks.

A swap is a contract to exchange one set of cash flows for another, where the flows depend on some market variable. Because its value derives from that variable, a swap is a derivative.

The workhorse is the vanilla interest-rate swap: one party pays a fixed rate, the other pays a floating rate (historically 3-month LIBOR). Concretely — receive 2.80% for 30Y vs pay floating. That single position is economically identical to:

  • Buying a 30-year bond with a 2.80% coupon (you receive fixed), and
  • Selling a floating-rate savings account (you pay floating),
  • …but with no exchange of the underlying assets and none of the associated funding cost. That's the magic — same exposure, "no money down."

The Rosetta Stone — say it five ways

Every swap has two sides, and the desk has five names for each. This table is worth memorizing cold, because the rest of fixed income borrows this language:

Pay fixed / receive floating

  • = Short swaps
  • = Short fixed-coupon bonds
  • = Short duration
  • = "Payer"
  • = Borrowing
  • → profits if rates RISE

Receive fixed / pay floating

  • = Long swaps
  • = Long fixed-coupon bonds
  • = Long duration
  • = "Receiver"
  • = Lending
  • → profits if rates FALL
Plain-English anchor
Pay fixed = short duration = bet rates go up. Receive fixed = long duration = bet rates go down. When a later slide says "issuers receive in size," translate instantly: they're buying duration / betting rates fall, which pushes swap rates down. Half of "drivers of swap spreads" is just applying this one reflex.

Why the market is enormous

Swaps are self-funded, deeply liquid, and the curve extends far out the maturity spectrum — so the swap curve became the benchmark against which other fixed-income assets are valued. The scale is hard to overstate: the market in its various forms had ~$100 trillion notional outstanding versus roughly $14 trillion of US government public debt. Participation skews to hedge funds (~45%), then asset managers (~20%), financial issuers (~15%), bank portfolios (~10%), with insurers and foreign accounts rounding it out. Around 80% of gross notional is centrally cleared, and dealers plus major swap participants still drive ~74% of activity.

A note from 2026
This deck is from 2020 and uses 3M LIBOR as the floating leg. USD LIBOR was discontinued (the panel ended mid-2023), and the market standard floating index is now SOFR — a near-risk-free overnight rate, compounded. Everywhere you read "3M LIBOR" below, the mechanics are identical; just mentally swap in SOFR as the floating reference. The deck already saw this coming — note OIS/SOFR sitting in the swap taxonomy in Section 4.
SECTION 02

How a swap is priced — “why par?”

A new swap costs nothing to enter. That isn't luck; it's arithmetic. Here's exactly why the two legs are worth the same dollar amount on day one.

Picture a standard 2Y USD pay-fixed swap as two bonds back-to-back:

  • You sell a 2-year fixed-rate bond: pay the coupon C/2 semi-annually. This leg has negative value to you (you owe it).
  • You buy a 2-year floating-rate bond: receive 3M floating, quarterly. This leg has positive value.
  • At inception the two legs are equal in magnitude by design — so the net value is zero. (Conventions: USD pays fixed 30/360 semi-annual, floating Act/360 quarterly.)
The "why par?" insight — this is the whole trick
A floating-rate bond always prices to par ($100) at a reset, because its coupon is the discount rate — they reset together, so they cancel. If the floating leg is worth par, then for the swap to be zero-cost the fixed leg must also be worth par. The fixed coupon that makes a fixed bond worth exactly par is, by definition, the par swap rate. So: "the swap rate is just the coupon that values the fixed leg at 100, matching the float leg's 100." That's all a swap rate is.

One wrinkle: OIS discounting

Cleared swaps are collateralized, so their cash flows are discounted at the overnight (OIS) rate, not LIBOR. That tiny change breaks the clean "$100 = $100." Worked example from the deck: a swap built to receive (OIS + 35bp) ends up with each leg worth $100.69, not $100.

  • Why the 35bp? It comes from the 2Y LIBOR/Fed-funds basis swap — the market price of LIBOR over the overnight rate.
  • Why a premium (the extra $0.69)? Because the spread over OIS is positive: the LIBOR coupons you receive are higher than the OIS rates you discount them at. Discounting bigger coupons at smaller rates lifts present value above par.

SECTION 03 · live

Carry & slide — the cost of just holding

Even if rates never move, a swap position makes or loses money over time. That's carry and slide — and once you can read them off the curve, you understand half of trade selection.

Carry is coupon income minus financing cost. Slide (or roll-down) is the gain/loss from your swap aging into a different point on the curve. Both fall straight out of the curve's geometry. Using a 10Y receiver over a 3-month horizon:

no-arbitrage: Yield(10Y) + Carry(3M) = Yield(3M×9Y9M) Carry(3M) = Yield(3M×9Y9M) − Yield(10Y) // the forward minus the spot roll-down: slide(3M) = Yield(10Y) − Yield(9Y9M) // your 10Y is a 9Y9M in 3 months

Add them and the middle terms telescope into a clean breakeven over a one-year horizon:

[Y(1Y×9Y) − Y(10Y)] + [Y(10Y) − Y(9Y)] = Y(1Y×9Y) − Y(9Y) └──── carry ────┘ └──── slide ────┘ └─ carry + slide ─┘

The deck's headline number: an investor receiving fixed in a 10Y expects to earn about 27bp over a year if the curve simply stays put. So being long 10s carries positively; shorts (paying fixed) are negative-carry. One honest caveat the authors stress: it's valid to read carry+slide as a breakeven move in spot yields; reading it as projected P/L is a stretch.

SECTION 04

The swap zoo

"Swap" is a family, not a single animal. Here's the taxonomy, split by what trades against what.

Fixed / Floating

  • • LIBOR swaps — fixed vs term LIBOR (legacy)
  • • OIS — fixed vs a compounded overnight index
  •    – SOFR and Fed funds are the overnight indices

Floating / Floating

  • • Tenor basis — one LIBOR tenor vs another (1s/3s, 3s/6s)
  • • Index basis — FF/LIBOR, SOFR/LIBOR, FF/SOFR
  • • Cross-currency basis — float in one currency vs another, + notional exchange
InstrumentWhat it does
FRA (forward rate agreement)Pays the difference between a reference rate on a future date and a pre-agreed rate; settles at the start of the period
Tenor basis swapSwaps one LIBOR tenor for another ± a fixed spread
Cross-currency basisExchanges notional at an agreed FX rate, each leg pays its own currency's index ± a spread
OISFixed vs a compounded overnight index (SOFR, FF); usually paid annually

The point of the zoo: each "basis" is a price for a specific risk — credit (LIBOR vs OIS), tenor, currency. Those bases are the raw material from which swap spreads are built.
SECTION 05

Swap spreads — what they are

Now the centerpiece. A swap spread compares the swap curve to the government curve — and it can be sliced several ways, each with its own quirk.

A swap spread pairs a cash security (usually a Treasury) with a duration-equivalent interest-rate swap. Loosely: swap spread ≈ swap rate − Treasury yield of matched maturity. It can be positive or negative, and it's a clean read on the swap market's value relative to risk-free govvies. But "matched" hides choices:

ConstructionHow it's matched
Maturity-matchedSwap maturity = security maturity
Par / parSwap coupon set to match the security's fixed coupon
TrueBoth coupon and maturity matched
Futures invoice spreadTreasury futures vs a forward-starting swap dated to the likely cheapest-to-deliver (CTD)
TSY / OISTreasury swapped to a maturity-matched OIS rate
In calm markets these track each other closely; in stress (e.g. March 2020) they can diverge sharply — which is itself tradable information.
Why does the spread even exist / move?
It's a tug-of-war. On one side, structural receivers of fixed (issuers, convexity hedgers) push swap rates down relative to Treasuries → spreads tighten/narrow. On the other, Treasury supply and the players who absorb it push the other way. Section 6 maps every one of these forces.
SECTION 06 · live

What moves swap spreads

The deck organizes the whole universe of flows into receivers vs the cash/supply side — then fits a regression that you can actually drive yourself.

The map of structural flows

Receivers (push spreads tighter)

  • • Issuance-related: domestic financials, Yankee banks, corporates issue fixed bonds and receive fixed in swaps
  • • Convexity hedgers: variable annuities, mortgage hedgers (servicers, banks, mREITs), callable-issuance hedges, systematic gamma shorts

Cash / supply side (push the other way)

  • • Treasury: net debt issuance (more govvies → wider/cheaper Treasuries → narrower spreads)
  • • Liability-driven investors (pensions)
  • • The Fed: asset purchases, funding-market interventions

Force #1 — issuance-related receiving

A financial issuer wants floating-rate liabilities (to match floating assets — classic asset/liability management). So it issues a fixed bond to investors, then receives fixed in a swap with the dealer to flip its own payments back to floating. The receiving leg of that swap pulls swap rates down → spreads tighten. Because issuance clusters in the calendar, 10Y swap spreads show robust seasonal patterns — certain weeks reliably tighten.

Force #2 — convexity hedging (the dangerous one)

Mortgages, MSRs and variable annuities carry negative convexity: their durations lengthen as rates rise and shorten as rates fall. To stay hedged, holders must trade in the same direction as the market — selling duration (paying fixed) into a sell-off, buying it (receiving) into a rally. When a big sell-off forces many hedgers to pay at once, it can snowball into a "convexity event" that whips spreads around. The deck flags that banks and mortgage REITs came into Q2 2020 unusually short, with the mortgage universe having re-loaded negative convexity.

Force #3 — supply vs the Fed

Heading into a record budget deficit, Treasury issuance was set to outpace Fed buying. More government supply cheapens Treasuries relative to swaps, giving longer-dated spreads room to narrow. The deck packages all of this into a regression of 6-month changes in the 30Y matched-maturity swap spread. The model below is that regression — drag any driver and watch the projected spread move.

SECTION 07 · the payoff

The hidden option in a flattener

The deck's most elegant idea. A perfectly boring-looking curve trade — pay 30Y, receive 50Y, sized to be duration-neutral — turns out to behave like a long option when rates move in parallel. Here's why, and you can dial it yourself.

Set up a duration-neutral long-end flattener: pay fixed in the 30Y and receive fixed in the 50Y, weighting the legs so their DV01s cancel. Now shock the whole curve up or down in parallel:

  • The duration exposure is engineered to zero — first-order, the trade doesn't care which way rates go.
  • But the 50Y has more convexity than the 30Y. Receiving the higher-convexity leg and paying the lower-convexity leg (matched on duration, not convexity) leaves you net long convexity.
  • Long convexity means a curved, smiling payoff: you make money whether rates rise or fall. That's exactly an option's profile — like owning a strangle, paid for with a little negative carry.
The math, in one line
Each leg's price moves as ΔP ≈ −D·Δy + ½·C·Δy². Weight the 30Y leg by w = D₅₀/D₃₀ so durations cancel, and the combined flattener payoff is pure convexity:
Payoff(Δy) = ½ · ( C₅₀ − (D₅₀/D₃₀)·C₃₀ ) · Δy² − carry drag
Because convexity grows faster than duration as maturity extends, that bracket is positive → a smile. The "carry drag" is what you pay (in negative carry + slide) to own this free convexity, and it sets the breakevens.

SECTION 08

Recap & self-test

The whole deck distilled, then four questions. Answer them and you've got the foundations cold.

  • A swap exchanges fixed for floating cash flows. Pay fixed = short duration = bet rates rise; receive fixed = long duration = bet rates fall.
  • Why par: the floating leg prices to 100 (coupon = discount rate), so the fixed coupon — the swap rate — is set to value the fixed leg at 100 too. Net cost zero. OIS discounting nudges this slightly (the +$0.69 example).
  • Carry + slide = what you earn/lose if the curve stays still; read it as a breakeven, not a forecast. Long 10s ≈ +27bp/yr positive carry on an upward curve.
  • A swap spread = swap rate − Treasury yield, matched several ways. It's a tug-of-war between structural receivers (issuers, convexity hedgers → tighter) and supply (Treasury → wider).
  • Drivers fit a regression (GC/OIS, curve, debt growth, VA hedging, Fed holdings) explaining ~72% of 30Y spread changes.
  • The hidden option: a duration-neutral long-end flattener is net long convexity, so it smiles under parallel shocks — an option you pay for in carry.
