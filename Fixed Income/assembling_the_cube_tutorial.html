Assembling the Cube

A handful of quotes go in the top. A dense, queryable, arbitrage-aware block of volatility falls out the side. This is the factory floor — every belt, every gear, every safety check.

In the first guide we met each machine on its own — cap stripping, the smile, SABR. Now we bolt them together and ask the two questions the caption quietly buried: what does it actually mean for the cube to be fully-queryable, and what stops it from being a contradiction machine — what makes it arbitrage-aware? We'll answer both with numbers you can poke.

★ The factory, in one breath

Picture a sausage factory, but for volatility. Raw meat (a few dozen market quotes) goes in. It passes down a belt: a grinder (cap stripping), a shaper (the smile), a filler that smooths it into a continuous loaf (expiry/tenor interpolation), and a patcher for the end-bits (lifting/basis/shift). Out the side comes a solid block you can slice anywhere — ask it for any expiry, any tenor, any strike, and it hands you a price. And a quality inspector at the end makes sure no two slices ever contradict each other (no free money). That inspector is the "arbitrage-aware" part, and almost nobody explains it. We will.

Chapter 01

The miracle, counted

Before admiring the factory, let's measure how much it actually invents. The word "sparse" and the word "dense" only land once you count.

A typical USD input set, the kind on those ICAP screens:

  • ~13 cap maturities × ~5 fixed strikes + an ATM column ≈ ~80 cap quotes.
  • ~10 swaption expiries × ~9 tenors for ATM ≈ ~90 swaption quotes, plus maybe a sparse OTM grid.

Call it ~200 numbers. That's the entire raw input. Now count what a trading desk needs to query:

Numerical reality check

The three axes are continuous. A single exotic — say a Bermudan swaption — might need vols at dozens of expiries, several tenors, and a whole range of strikes for its calibration. If you discretised the cube even crudely at, say, 40 expiries × 40 tenors × 40 strikes, that's 64,000 points. In reality the cube must answer for any real number on each axis — uncountably many.

So the factory's compression ratio is roughly 200 inputs → ∞ outputs. Every output beyond the 200 is manufactured — and must not contradict the 200, or each other.

The cube is not a table of answers. It is a machine that fabricates an answer on demand for a point nobody ever quoted.

Chapter 03

Stage 1 — Cap stripping, peeled with real numbers

The grinder. Market caps quote a single "term" vol per maturity, but each cap secretly contains all the shorter caps' caplets. To get the atomic caplet (forward) vols, we peel: a longer cap minus the shorter cap = just the new caplets.

Watch the arithmetic, layer by layer

Let's do it with actual numbers. Annual caplets, forward and strike both 3% (ATM), discount factor 1. The market hands us flat cap vols. We price each cap, subtract, and back out the new layer's vol. (Black caplet price uses the formula from Guide I.)

Chapter 04

Stage 2 — The smile fills the strike axis

After stripping we have caplet vols at a few strikes for each maturity — still a comb of discrete points. Stage 2 runs a smile model (PWL, CEV, or SABR — see Guide I) to turn that comb into a continuous curve $\sigma(K)$, and to extrapolate sensibly past the last quote.

Numerical example — SABR at one node

Take a 5y caplet, forward $F=3\%$. Fit SABR with $\alpha=0.027,\ \beta=0.5,\ \rho=0,\ \nu=0.45$. It returns, for instance, $\sigma(2\%)\!\approx\!21.5\%$, $\sigma(3\%)\!\approx\!16.9\%$ (ATM), $\sigma(5\%)\!\approx\!19.3\%$ — a full grin from four parameters. The cube now has $\sigma(K)$ for every strike at this node, not just the quoted ones. [matches Guide I's SABR lab]

Do this at every $(T,\tau)$ node and you've filled the entire strike axis. Two axes left.

Chapter 05

Stage 3 — Bilinear on moneyness, derived & worked

Now the filler. We have a smile at each grid node $(T_i,\tau_j)$, but a query lands between nodes. We blend the four surrounding smiles with bilinear interpolation — weighting each corner by how close it is — but evaluated at matching moneyness, not absolute strike.

Deriving the four weights

Bilinear interpolation from scratch
Locate the query between grid lines. Define fractional positions along each axis:
$$ w_T=\frac{T^*-T_i}{T_{i+1}-T_i},\qquad w_\tau=\frac{\tau^*-\tau_j}{\tau_{j+1}-\tau_j} $$
$w_T=0$ means "sitting on the left expiry," $w_T=1$ means "on the right." Just "how far across the box am I."
Interpolate first in expiry (two blends), then in tenor (one blend). Composing the two linear steps multiplies the fractions:
$$ w_{00}=(1-w_T)(1-w_\tau),\;\; w_{10}=w_T(1-w_\tau),\;\; w_{01}=(1-w_T)w_\tau,\;\; w_{11}=w_T w_\tau $$
Each weight is the area of the opposite rectangle. The corner diagonally across from you gets the biggest slice — being far from a corner along both axes means that corner barely matters.
The weights automatically sum to 1 (expand and watch it collapse):
$$ \sum w = (1-w_T)(1-w_\tau)+w_T(1-w_\tau)+(1-w_T)w_\tau+w_T w_\tau = 1 $$
No leakage — the blend is a true weighted average, so the result always lands between the four corner vols. That's what keeps interpolation from inventing nonsense.
The moneyness twist: don't read each corner's smile at the raw strike $K^*$. Read it at its own strike that matches the query's distance-from-ATM:
$$ K_{ij}-F_{ij}=K^*-F^*\quad\Longrightarrow\quad K_{ij}=F_{ij}+(K^*-F^*) $$
A smile cares about "how far out of the money," not the raw rate. If you blended at the same absolute strike, you'd be comparing the ATM region of one corner with the deep wing of another — smearing the skew. Align on moneyness first.
Worked example — one query by hand

Query T*=0.7y, τ*=3y. Grid brackets: expiries 0.5y & 1y, tenors 2y & 5y. Then

w_T = (0.7−0.5)/(1−0.5) = 0.40,   w_τ = (3−2)/(5−2) = 0.333

Chapter 06

Stage 4 — Lifting, basis & shift: the patchers

Stages 1–3 assume the data is reasonably complete. Real markets are full of holes and quirks; Stage 4 is three patchers that keep the loaf intact (all covered in depth in Guide I, recapped here as where they sit in the line):

  • Smile lifting — when a swaption has an ATM vol but no OTM quotes, borrow a same-expiry caplet's smile shape and re-anchor it. Fills missing smile shapes.
  • Basis model — when two index tenors (3m vs 6m) carry a stubborn spread, scale Black vol by $1/F$ so absolute wobble stays invariant. Fixes frequency mismatch.
  • Shift / normal space — when rates go negative, slide everything right (shifted SABR) or switch to normal vol. Keeps the machine legal below zero.
▲ Why these come last

They're conditional — invoked only when a query hits a hole or a non-standard corner. The healthy interior of the cube never touches them. They're the patch kit, not the main belt.

◈ What "queryable" buys you

An exotic-derivative model never asks the market a question. It asks the cube — thousands of times, at oddball points — and trusts that each answer is consistent with the original 200 quotes. The query engine you're playing with is, in miniature, what runs under every exotic valuation on the desk.

Chapter 08

"Arbitrage-aware": the inspector at the end

Here's the phrase nobody explains. A cube can be smooth, dense and queryable and still be broken — if its prices secretly let someone make risk-free money. The factory needs a quality inspector. To build one, we need to know what "no free money" means in the language of a smile.

The deepest free lunch: the butterfly

Buy one call at strike $K-\Delta$, sell two at $K$, buy one at $K+\Delta$. This "butterfly" can never have negative value — its payoff is a little tent that's zero or positive everywhere. So the call price $C(K)$ must be convex in strike. And convexity has a stunning consequence, discovered by Breeden & Litzenberger:

$\partial^2 C/\partial K^2$ equals the risk-neutral density — reproduced numerically to 4 decimals against the lognormal pdf.
Breeden–Litzenberger: the smile hides a probability density
A call's value is the expected payoff over the (risk-neutral) density $\phi$ of the future rate $F_T$:
$$ C(K) = \int_K^\infty (x-K)\,\phi(x)\,dx $$
Sum "how much each outcome pays" times "how likely it is." Only outcomes above $K$ pay.
Differentiate once in $K$ (Leibniz rule — the boundary term vanishes since the integrand is 0 at $x=K$):
$$ \frac{\partial C}{\partial K} = -\int_K^\infty \phi(x)\,dx = -\,\Pr(F_T>K) $$
The slope of the call-price-vs-strike curve is (minus) the probability of finishing in the money. Already useful — but watch the next step.
Differentiate again — the fundamental theorem of calculus hands you the density itself:
$$ \boxed{\;\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} = \phi(K)\;} $$
The curvature of the price-vs-strike curve is the probability density of the rate landing at $K$. The market's smile literally encodes a probability distribution.
A probability density can never be negative. Therefore the no-arbitrage condition is simply:
$$ \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} \ge 0 \quad\text{everywhere.} $$
If a smile is so curved (or so steep) that the implied density dips below zero, the cube is asserting a "negative probability" — pure nonsense, and an exploitable arbitrage. That's the inspector's red light.

The other inspector: calendar arbitrage

There's a second free lunch across time. A longer-dated option must cost at least as much as a shorter-dated one (more time = more chance to pay off). In vol language, the total implied variance $w = \sigma^2 T$ must never decrease as expiry grows, at fixed moneyness:

CALENDAR $$ \frac{\partial}{\partial T}\big[\sigma^2(T)\,T\big] \ge 0 $$
◈ Why variance, not vol

Vol itself can fall with maturity (long-dated vols are often lower) — that's fine. What can't happen is total accumulated uncertainty $\sigma^2 T$ going backwards, because uncertainty only piles up with time. The cube's flat-in-time extrapolation (Guide I, Ch 9) respects this: holding $\sigma$ flat makes $\sigma^2T$ strictly increasing.

⚠ Honest limits

"Arbitrage-aware" does not mean "provably arbitrage-free everywhere." SABR's asymptotic formula is famous for admitting tiny butterfly arbitrage in extreme low-strike wings — the very reason shifted/normal SABR and density monitors exist. The cube's job is to stay consistent with the quoted region and flag trouble outside it, not to be perfect at $\pm\infty$.

Chapter 09

The living cube

One last reframe. The cube is not built once and shelved — it's recalibrated continuously as quotes tick. Every refresh re-runs the whole assembly line: re-strip, re-fit smiles, re-interpolate, re-check arbitrage. The 200 inputs change by a basis point; the entire manufactured interior shifts to stay consistent.

So when a trader pulls up VCUB <GO> and reads a vol for some bespoke 7-month-into-3.6-year swaption struck 40bp out of the money, they're reading the live output of this exact pipeline — stripped, smiled, blended on moneyness, patched if needed, and silently inspected for free lunches. A few raisins; a whole cake; and an inspector making sure every bite agrees.

Sparse in. Dense out. Callable anywhere. Contradicting nowhere. That is the cube.
Appendix

The four derivations

A · The cap-stripping bootstrap

From "caps contain caps" to a one-unknown solve
A cap of maturity $T_n$ is the sum of all its caplets; a shorter cap $T_{n-1}$ is a subset. Subtract:
$$ P_n(K)-P_{n-1}(K)=\sum_{t_i=T_{n-1}}^{t_iEverything the two caps share cancels, leaving only the caplets in the new time band.
Assume those new caplets share one (piecewise-constant) vol $\sigma_n$. Then the right side is a known function of a single unknown:
$$ P_n-P_{n-1}=\sum_{i} p_i(K,\sigma_n) $$
One equation, one unknown ⇒ invert (bisection on $\sigma_n$). March $n=1,2,3,\dots$ and you've stripped the whole curve — the Chapter 3 table.

B · Bilinear weights

Derived in Chapter 5: $w_{ij}$ are products of the axis fractions $w_T,w_\tau$, equal to the area of the opposite sub-rectangle, and sum to 1. The moneyness alignment $K_{ij}=F_{ij}+(K^*-F^*)$ ensures corners are compared at equal distance-from-ATM.

C · Breeden–Litzenberger

Derived in Chapter 8: writing the call as $\int_K^\infty(x-K)\phi(x)dx$ and differentiating twice in $K$ gives $\partial^2C/\partial K^2=\phi(K)$. Non-negativity of $\phi$ is the no-butterfly condition. (With discounting, $\partial^2C/\partial K^2 = e^{-rT}\phi(K)$ — same sign test.)

D · The calendar condition

Why total variance must rise
At fixed moneyness, a longer option dominates a shorter one (it can do everything the shorter can, plus more time):
$$ C(T_2)\ge C(T_1)\quad\text{for }T_2>T_1 $$
More time can only add optionality, never remove it — else buy the long, sell the short, pocket the difference.
In Black terms the price depends on $T$ only through total variance $w=\sigma^2T$. Monotone price ⇒ monotone variance:
$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial T}\big(\sigma^2 T\big)\ge 0 $$
Flat-in-time vol extrapolation (constant $\sigma$) gives $\partial_T(\sigma^2T)=\sigma^2>0$ automatically — which is exactly why the cube extrapolates flat in expiry.
