How to bet on the curve
only when you're right about direction.

A conditional trade is a yield-curve position that only switches on if rates move the way you predicted — and costs nothing to put on. This notebook builds the whole idea from zero, with live numbers you can poke.

EXHIBIT 01

The building blocks

Four ideas you need before anything else makes sense: a swap, the yield curve, what "steepening" means, and PVBP. We'll define each like you've never seen it.

A swap, in one breath

An interest-rate swap is a contract where two people exchange interest payments. One side pays a fixed rate (locked in today), the other pays a floating rate (resets with the market). When a trader says "I pay 10Y swaps," they mean: I agreed to pay the fixed 10-year rate. If rates go up after that, paying a now-cheap fixed rate is a win — so paying fixed = betting rates go up. Receiving fixed is the opposite bet.

Why this matters
Everything in this paper is built from "pay" and "receive." Pay = profit if yields rise. Receive = profit if yields fall. Tattoo that on your arm and the rest follows.

The yield curve

Different maturities have different rates. Plot the rate for 1Y, 2Y, 3Y … 30Y and you get the yield curve. We name a slope by its two ends. 3s/20s means "the 20-year rate minus the 3-year rate" — the steepness between those two points, measured in basis points (bp), where 1bp = 0.01%.

  • The curve steepens when the long end rises relative to the short end — 3s/20s gets bigger.
  • The curve flattens when that gap shrinks — 3s/20s gets smaller.

Steepeners and flatteners (the outright trade)

A 3s/20s steepener profits when the curve steepens. You build it by paying the 20Y (betting the long end rises) and receiving the 3Y (betting the short end falls, or rises less). Whatever direction yields go, you make money if the gap widens. A flattener is the mirror image.

Plain-English intuition
An outright steepener is a pure bet on the shape of the curve. It does not care whether rates went up or down — only whether the 20Y pulled away from the 3Y. That sounds great, until you realize you're also exposed when the curve does the "right" shape move for the "wrong" reason. That gap is exactly the hole conditional trades plug.

PVBP — the unit of risk

PVBP (price value of a basis point; also called DV01) answers: "If this swap's yield moves 1bp, how many dollars/euros do I make or lose?" Longer swaps have bigger PVBP because a 1bp move compounds over more years.

From the paper's example: a 3-year swap has PVBP = 301 and a 20-year swap has PVBP = 1790 (per €mn). So a 20Y swap is 1790 / 301 ≈ 5.95× as sensitive to a 1bp move as a 3Y swap.

The risk-weighting rule
To make a curve trade "balanced" (a pure slope bet, neutral to parallel moves), you hold more of the less-sensitive leg. If the long leg notional is 100, the short leg notional is 100 × (PVBPL/PVBPS) = 100 × 1790/301 ≈ 595. Now a 1bp move in both legs nets to roughly zero P&L — only the difference (the curve) moves your book. This "595 vs 100" pairing shows up everywhere below, so remember where it came from.
EXHIBIT 02

Swaptions: turning swaps into options

A conditional trade is built from options on swaps. Here's the entire vocabulary, with the one mathematical fact that makes the whole structure work.

A swaption is the right (not obligation) to enter a swap later, at a rate fixed today (the strike). Two flavours:

  • Payer swaption — the right to pay fixed. It pays off if rates rise above the strike. A payer is a bet that yields go up.
  • Receiver swaption — the right to receive fixed. It pays off if rates fall below the strike. A receiver is a bet that yields go down.

The notation 3Mx20Y means: an option expiring in 3 months ("3M", the expiry), on a 20-year swap ("20Y", the tail).

Strikes: ATMF, ITM, OTM

  • ATMF (at-the-money-forward): the strike equals the rate the market currently expects (the forward rate). The standard "fair" starting point.
  • ITM (in-the-money): the option already has value — rates have moved your way.
  • OTM (out-of-the-money): rates must move further before it's worth anything. Cheaper, because it needs more help.

Implied volatility — priced in "bp/day"

Implied volatility (σ) is the market's price for uncertainty: how much the underlying rate is expected to wiggle. Rates desks quote it in basis points per day. In the paper's example, the 3Y option's vol is σS = 25.0 bp/day and the 20Y option's is σL = 63.4 bp/day. The long end is far more volatile — that asymmetry is the engine of the whole trade.

The one fact that makes everything work
For an ATMF option, the premium (its cost) is almost exactly linear in volatility:
Poption  =  σ × PVBP × √( T / (2π) )   // price as % of notional
Read it slowly: cost goes up with volatility (σ), up with sensitivity (PVBP), and up with the square root of time to expiry (T). Because the relationship with σ is a straight multiply, you can perfectly cancel two ATMF premiums by adjusting either σ-driven price or notional. Hold this thought for Section 5 — it's why "premium neutral at zero cost" is even possible.

Delta (δ) and gamma (Γ) round out the toolkit: delta is how fast the option's value changes as the rate moves; gamma is how fast the delta itself changes. They appear once, in the strike-shift formula, and we'll explain them right there.

EXHIBIT 03

The conditional idea

Now we can state the actual trick — and see exactly when it pays and when it quietly expires worthless.

Here's the observation the whole paper rests on. In Europe, with the central bank's policy rate pinned down, the curve has been "directional" with the level of yields: it steepens when rates sell off (rise) and flattens when rates rally (fall).

Plain-English intuition
If you believe "the curve will steepen and it'll happen in a sell-off," why pay for steepening in a rally too? An outright steepener gives you the shape exposure in all directions. A conditional steepener gives it to you only in the sell-off you actually predicted — and because you give up the other scenarios, you can usually get it for free.

How you build a conditional bear-steepener

"Bear" = you expect a sell-off (rates up). "Steepener" = you expect the curve to steepen. So you want upside-to-rates exposure that's stronger on the long end. Payers give upside-to-rates exposure. So:

BUY 3Mx20Y payer // long the volatile long end — wins big if 20Y rises SELL 3Mx3Y payer // short the quiet short end — funds the trade

You're long the 20Y payer and short the 3Y payer, risk-weighted 1:1 (notionals 100 vs 595, from Section 1). Now trace the four things rates can do:

Sell-off, curve steepens ✓

Both payers go in-the-money, but the 20Y leg you're long gains more than the 3Y leg you're short loses. Net profit. This is your thesis paying off.

Rally, curve flattens

Rates fall, so both payers expire worthless. You gave nothing, you get nothing back. Flat P&L — the downside is capped at zero because you set it up at zero cost.

Sell-off, but curve barely steepens

Both options go ITM, but the steepening is less than the option prices already implied. The short leg's loss eats the long leg's gain. Small loss.

Bull-steepen (curve right, direction wrong)

Curve steepens but in a rally. Your payers expire worthless and you miss it entirely. An outright steepener would have caught this; the conditional one won't. That's the price of the discount.

The asymmetry you're buying
Heads (your scenario): you profit. Tails (rally): you lose nothing. The whole game is to get that payoff for zero up-front cost, and to make sure the curve has been steepening in sell-offs by more than the option market is charging you for. That second condition is the next section.
EXHIBIT 04 · live

Implied vs delivered directionality

This is the single most important number pair in the paper. Get this and you understand why the trade has edge — not just how it's built.

The option market already knows the long end is more volatile, and charges for it. So part of the directionality is "priced in." The question is whether the market is charging enough. Two measures:

Definitions (memorize these two lines)

Implied directionality = how much the option market thinks the curve is directional:

Implied dir  =  1 − ( σS / σL )   // short-leg vol ÷ long-leg vol

Delivered directionality = how directional the curve actually was:

Delivered dir  =  1 − β   // β = how much the 3Y moves per 1bp move in the 20Y

Here β (beta) is the realized sensitivity of the short-end yield to the long-end yield. If the 20Y moves 10bp and the 3Y only moves 2bp, then β = 0.2, so delivered directionality = 1 − 0.2 = 0.8 (very directional — the long end does almost all the moving, which is exactly what steepens the curve in a sell-off).

The trade rule, in one sentence
The conditional trade is attractive when delivered directionality is bigger than implied directionality — i.e. the curve has been moving more directionally than the option market is charging you for. The gap, (delivered − implied), is your edge.

EXHIBIT 05

Two ways to make it free

A balanced curve trade is premium-neutral only if the two legs' vols are identical — which they almost never are (σL > σS). So we force it to zero cost one of two ways. This is the heart of the paper.

Here's the problem in numbers. If you trade both legs at ATMF with 1:1 risk weights, the premiums don't match:

Leg3Mx3Y (short)3Mx20Y (long)
PVBP3011790
Implied vol (bp/day)25.063.4
ATMF strike−0.10%1.10%
ATMF premium (bp of notional)15234
Source: the paper's Exhibit 8. The long-leg option costs far more — both because it's more sensitive (PVBP) and more volatile (σ). We must neutralize that.

Recipe A — Strike Shift (keep 1:1 risk, move the strike)

Keep the clean 1:1 risk weights (notional 100 long / 595 short — a true curve trade). To kill the cost difference, push the expensive leg's strike out-of-the-money until its premium drops to match the cheap leg you sold. In the example the 20Y strike moves up 23bp, from 1.10% to 1.33%. Now both legs cost the same and net to zero.

The catch (this is the give-up)
Because your long 20Y is now struck 23bp OTM, the curve must steepen by at least ~23bp beyond the forward, with yields rising, before you make a cent. Your entry level is 143bp (= strike 1.33% − strike −0.10%) versus a forward curve around 119bp — a ~23bp "give-up." You kept full 1:1 curve exposure, but you start deep in a hole.

Recipe B — Weighted Curve (keep ATMF, shrink the notional)

Leave both options at ATMF (no hole to climb out of). Instead, buy less of the expensive leg. Since ATMF premium is linear in vol, reducing the long-leg notional by the vol ratio W = σS/σL = 25/63.4 ≈ 0.39 makes the two premiums match exactly. Watch it balance:

cost(long) = notional × premium = (100×0.39) × 234 = 39 × 234 ≈ 9100 cost(short) = 595 × 15 ≈ 8900 // they match → zero net cost
The catch (this is the leverage)
Your entry level is just 53bp — far better than 143bp! But there's a string attached: you no longer own the real 3s/20s curve. You own a vol-weighted curve: W × 20Y − 3Y = 0.39 × 20Y − 3Y. That changes how the trade behaves as rates move — which is the entire point of the simulator next.

The strike-shift formula (Recipe A's math)

How big must the strike shift x be? You need the option's price to drop by ΔP (the gap between the two ATMF premiums). Using delta (δ) and gamma (Γ) as a 2-term Taylor approximation of how price falls as you move the strike OTM:

δ·(−x) + 0.5·Γ·(−x)² = −ΔP // (eq. 1) x = [ δ ± √( δ² − 4·(0.5·Γ·ΔP) ) ] / ( 2·0.5·Γ ) // (eq. 2)

It's just the quadratic formula. δ captures the first-order price drop per bp of strike move; the Γ term is the curvature correction. (Exact when the vol surface is flat across strikes; an approximation when there's skew.) You don't need to compute this by hand — but now when you see equation (2) in the paper, you know it's "solve a quadratic for the strike shift."

Simulator: which recipe wins?

Both recipes are free at inception and exploit the same edge. But they pay out completely differently as rates move — because one owns the real curve and the other owns the weighted curve. This is a faithful, interactive rebuild of the paper's Exhibits 14–17.

What you're looking at — the slopes decoded

Each line is just two option payoffs added together. Once both options are in-the-money, the slopes are fixed and simple:

  • Weighted curve climbs at slope W (only long ITM), then at W − β once the short leg kicks in. If β grows past W, that second slope turns negative — the weighted curve has "flattened" against you.
  • Strike-shift first dips at slope −β (you're short the 3Y, which goes ITM before your OTM 20Y does), then rips upward at slope 1 − β once the 20Y finally clears its strike. It starts in a hole but has the steepest upside.
The punchline — and why the breakeven is fixed
Set the two "both-ITM" payoffs equal and the β term cancels on both sides, leaving:
x*  =  δ / ( 1 − W ) + CL  ≈  23/(1−0.39) + 2.3 ≈ 40 bp
The crossover between the two strategies is independent of how directional the move turns out to be (β) — try it: move the β slider and the breakeven stays at 40. It depends only on the strike shift δ, the vol ratio W, and carry. So:
  • Small moves (x below ~40bp): the Weighted curve wins — it's already making money while strike-shift is still climbing out of its hole.
  • Big sell-offs (x above ~40bp, e.g. a "Bund VaR shock"): the Strike-shift wins — its 1:1 exposure and steep 1−β slope overtake the leveraged-but-capped weighted curve.
  • Limiting case: if σS = σL (W=1), no strike shift is needed (δ=0) and the two recipes become identical. Set W near 0.85 and δ near 0 in the sim and watch the lines merge.

Model note: this reproduces the paper's exhibits using its example parameters with small, fixed illustrative carries (CL≈CS≈2.3bp) so the kinks are visible. The shapes, slopes, and the 40bp breakeven match Exhibits 14–17 exactly; precise P&L magnitudes depend on the exact carry assumptions.

EXHIBIT 07

Same trick, four more markets

Once you see the pattern — "trade two options, force zero cost, exploit implied vs delivered directionality" — it generalizes. The paper points at four extensions.

1 · Cross-market trades

Instead of two maturities in one currency, use the same maturity in two currencies. Example from the paper: USD − EUR at 1Yx1Y. If USD is expected to sell off harder than EUR, you buy the USD payer and sell the EUR payer. EUR vol was about 40% of USD vol, and the USD-vs-EUR spread had widened in sell-offs by more than that ratio implied — same edge, different axis.

2 · Swap-spread trades

A swap spread is the gap between a swap yield and a government-bond yield (e.g. Bobl swap spreads vs Bobl yields). When that spread is directional with the underlying yield but the option market underprices it, you express the spread view conditionally — exactly the machinery above, relabeled.

3 · Midcurve structures

Options on forward-starting swaps ("reds," "greens," "blues" in futures slang). You can run a conditional steepener of, say, the reds/blues curve, or midcurves versus regular swaptions. Mechanics unchanged.

4 · Conditional butterflies

A butterfly bets on curvature: long the body, short the two wings, on a 50/100/50 risk basis. A conditional fly profits if the fly richens/cheapens and your yield view is right. Both recipes still apply — for the Weighted version, the body's risk weight becomes:

Body weight = ( σLeft + σRight ) / ( 2 · σBody ) // wings stay at 50% each

You can even build market-neutral conditional flies, where the risk weights are set by empirical regression to strip out level and curve exposure, leaving a pure curvature bet — then strikes are shifted for zero cost.

EXHIBIT 08 · for the deep divers

Why the two recipes share one edge

The paper makes a beautiful claim: the number that tells you Recipe A is attractive (delivered − implied directionality) is mathematically the same as the number that tells you Recipe B is attractive (the regression beta of the weighted curve to yields). Here's the proof, slowed down.

Let σi = implied vol, σr = realized (delivered) vol, subscripts S/L for short/long. The regression beta of one yield on another can be written β(YS,YL) = ρ · σrS/σrL, where ρ is their correlation.

Recipe A's metric: delivered − implied
delivered − implied = [ 1 − ρ·(σrS/σrL) ] − [ 1 − (σiS/σiL) ] = σiS/σiL − ρ·(σrS/σrL) // (call this result ★)
Recipe B's metric: beta of the weighted curve to YL

The weighted curve is (σiS/σiL)·YL − YS. Regression beta is associative: β(A−B, X) = β(A,X) − β(B,X). So:

β( (σiS/σiL)·YL − YS , YL ) = (σiS/σiL)·β(YL,YL) − β(YS,YL) = σiS/σiL − ρ·(σrS/σrL) // identical to ★ ✓

The two expressions are the same symbol-for-symbol. That's why the paper says the methods are "fundamentally similar and exploit the discrepancy between implied and delivered directionality" — they're measuring the identical edge two ways. They only differ in how the P&L evolves once rates start moving, which is the Section 6 story.

So how do you choose?
Expect a small-to-moderate, persistent grind in your direction → Weighted curve (better entry, wins below the ~40bp breakeven). Expect a violent tail sell-off → Strike shift (full 1:1 exposure, wins above breakeven). When in doubt, the breakeven formula δ/(1−W) + CL tells you exactly where the handoff is.

The whole paper in six lines

  • A conditional curve trade = buy a payer/receiver on one tail, sell one on the other, so curve exposure switches on only in your predicted direction.
  • It's built for zero cost, so the wrong direction just expires flat.
  • Edge exists when delivered directionality > implied directionality (curve moved more directionally than options were charging).
  • Recipe A (Strike shift): keep 1:1 risk, push the dear leg OTM. Worse entry, but full real-curve exposure and the steepest upside.
  • Recipe B (Weighted curve): keep ATMF, shrink the dear leg's notional by the vol ratio. Better entry, but you own a vol-weighted curve that can cap out or reverse.
  • Crossover at x* = δ/(1−W) + CL ≈ 40bp, independent of β. Small move → B wins. Big sell-off → A wins. Equal vols → identical.
