Five forces, one number:
decoding the swap-spread regression.

The deck claims a handful of drivers explain ~72% of how the 30-year swap spread moves. This page takes that one model apart — what a regression is, why each driver pushes the spread the way it does, and a cockpit where you rebuild the −46 → −60 projection yourself.

Quick refresher so this lands: a 30Y swap spread = the 30-year swap rate minus the 30-year Treasury yield. It was around −46bp in 2020 (swap rate below Treasury). "Narrowing" here means going more negative (−46 → −60); "widening" means moving back up toward zero. Keep that direction in mind and everything below clicks.
PART 01

What a regression actually is

No stats background needed. A regression is just "a line of best fit through history that learns how much each thing matters."

Imagine plotting, for the last 10 years, every 6-month change in the swap spread against every 6-month change in (say) Treasury supply. You'd get a cloud of dots. A regression draws the single line that sits closest to all of them — and the slope of that line is your answer: "on average, when supply rose by 1 unit, the spread moved by this much."

With five drivers it's the same idea in five dimensions. The model spits out one number per driver — its coefficient — plus a baseline intercept. Put together:

predicted change in spread = intercept + coef₁ × (change in driver 1) + coef₂ × (change in driver 2) + … for all five drivers // that's the whole model
  • Coefficient = sensitivity. "For each +1 unit of this driver, the spread moves by coef bp, holding the others fixed." A negative coef means they move oppositely; a positive one means together.
  • Intercept = the baseline drift when every driver-change is zero — a built-in tendency the spread has on its own.
  • Each driver has its own units (bp, $bn, etc.), so the coefficients aren't comparable head-to-head. What matters is coef × how much that driver actually moved — its contribution.
The one mental model
The regression is a recipe: take how much each ingredient changed, multiply by how much that ingredient matters (its coefficient), add them all up plus a base amount (intercept), and you get the predicted move in the spread. The cockpit in Part 4 is this recipe, made draggable.

PART 03 · the heart

The five drivers, and why each one pushes

A coefficient with no story is just a number. Here's the economic chain behind each — the part that gives you real understanding. The chain that matters runs: a force makes Treasuries cheaper or richer → that changes (swap rate − Treasury yield).

The master reflex
The spread is swap − Treasury. So anything that makes Treasuries cheaper (yields up) pushes the spread more negative (narrows). Anything that makes Treasuries richer (yields down) pushes it less negative (widens). Every driver below is just a different reason Treasuries get cheaper or richer.

1 · Treasury supply — %chg WAM-weighted debt

coef −0.32
unit: growth in (debt × its maturity) — i.e. total duration being issued

When the government runs a huge deficit it floods the market with bonds. WAM-weighting (debt × maturity) captures not just how much but how long — long bonds carry more duration to digest. More supply than buyers want → bond prices fall → yields rise.

more Treasury supply → market must absorb it → Treasuries cheapen (yields ↑) → (swap − Tsy) ↓ narrows
2020: a record deficit meant debt growth of +14.3, the single biggest narrowing push: −0.32 × 14.3 = −4.6bp.

2 · Foreign demand — Fed custody holdings

coef +0.049
unit: $bn of Treasuries the Fed safekeeps for foreign central banks (a proxy for foreign official demand)

Foreign central banks park their Treasuries in a custody account at the Fed. When that balance rises, foreigners are buying Treasuries — strong demand richens them. This is the one driver with a positive coefficient: more demand → richer Treasuries → wider (less negative) spread.

foreign demand ↑ (custody ↑) → Treasuries richen (yields ↓) → (swap − Tsy) ↑ widens
2020: custody was expected to fall by 120 — foreign demand fading. A falling positive-coef driver narrows the spread: +0.049 × (−120) = −5.9bp, the biggest single push.

3 · Funding stress — 3M GC/OIS spread

coef −0.29
unit: bp premium of secured repo funding (GC) over the risk-free overnight rate (OIS)

GC/OIS measures how expensive it is to finance a Treasury in the repo market. When it widens, holding Treasuries costs more, so investors demand a higher yield to bother — Treasuries cheapen. It's a real-time gauge of plumbing stress.

repo funding dearer (GC/OIS ↑) → costlier to hold Treasuries → Treasuries cheapen → spread narrows
2020: a modest +5 widening assumed → −0.29 × 5 = −1.4bp.

4 · Insurer hedging — VA hedging demand

coef −0.121
unit: $bn of 20Y-equivalent duration that variable-annuity books need to hedge

Insurers who sold variable annuities have long-dated guarantees to hedge. They do it by receiving fixed in long swaps to add duration. More VA hedging demand = more receiving = it pushes the swap rate down (not the Treasury), so the spread narrows. This is a pure "structural receiver" force.

more VA hedging → insurers receive fixed in swaps → swap rate ↓ → (swap − Tsy) ↓ narrows
2020: assumed unchanged (0) → 0.0bp this time, but a powerful lever when it moves.

5 · Curve shape — 10s/30s Treasury curve

coef −0.417
unit: bp slope of the Treasury curve (30Y yield − 10Y yield)

This is partly a control variable: the long end of the Treasury curve and the 30Y spread tend to move together because both reflect long-end supply and duration pressure. When the curve steepens (the 30Y point cheapens relative to 10Y), 30Y spreads narrow alongside. Its t-stat (−47) is the largest — the tightest relationship in the model.

Treasury curve steepens (30Y cheapens) → 30Y underperforms swaps → spread narrows
2020: assumed unchanged (0) → 0.0bp, but it absorbs a lot of the co-movement that would otherwise look like noise.

PART 05

Can you trust the model?

Two numbers tell you how much to believe a regression: the t-stats (is each relationship real?) and R² (how much does the whole model explain?).

R² = 72%

Of all the wiggling in the 30Y spread, the model's five drivers explain about 72%. The points hug the fit line but don't sit on it — the leftover 28% is everything the model can't see (one-off flows, sentiment, noise). For a financial model, 72% is genuinely strong.

t-stats: 8 to 47

A t-stat says "how many standard errors away from zero is this relationship?" Above ~2 (the dashed line) it's unlikely to be a fluke. Every driver here clears it by miles — the curve term hits 47 — so these aren't accidental correlations.

What "std err = 6.4bp" means
It's the typical size of the model's miss. So the −60 projection is really "−60, give or take ~6bp." Useful for direction and rough magnitude — not a to-the-basis-point guarantee.
PART 06

The honest caveats

A good analyst trusts this model and knows exactly where it can mislead. Four things to hold in mind.

  • Changes, not levels. The model regresses 6-month changes against changes, not levels against levels. Spread levels drift over time, and regressing drifting things on each other invents fake correlations ("spurious regression"). Differencing strips the drift out — a deliberate, important choice.
  • Correlation isn't causation. The curve term especially is a co-mover/control, not a clean cause. The model captures relationships, which is enough to forecast — but don't over-read any single coefficient as a lever you could pull in isolation.
  • It's fit on the past. Coefficients are averages over a 10-year window. In a genuine regime shift (a new Fed reaction function, a funding crisis), the relationships can change and the model degrades until it re-learns.
  • The forecast is conditional. −60 only follows if the assumed driver changes happen. The deck itself hedges: "barring a significant acceleration in Fed purchases." The drivers can surprise — which is exactly why the cockpit lets you test other scenarios.
The right way to use it
Treat the output as a disciplined anchor: "given these supply and demand assumptions, fair value for the 30Y spread is around −60." If the market sits far from that, either the market is wrong (an opportunity) or your driver assumptions are (update them). The model organizes the debate; it doesn't end it.
PART 07

Recap & self-test

  • A regression learns one coefficient per driver: how much the spread moves per unit of that driver. Forecast = intercept + Σ(coef × change).
  • The master reflex: anything that cheapens Treasuries narrows the spread (more negative); anything that richens them widens it.
  • Supply (debt growth) and fading foreign demand (Fed custody falling) were the two big narrowing forces into year-end 2020.
  • Fed custody is the lone positive coefficient — more foreign buying = richer Treasuries = wider spread.
  • R² 72% = strong explanatory power; huge t-stats = real relationships; ±6bp typical miss.
  • Trust it as an anchor, not an oracle — it's changes-based, fit on the past, and conditional on your assumptions.
