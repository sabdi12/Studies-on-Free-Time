CHAPTER 01

What is a Treasury, and why does anyone care?

Before we can understand a "Treasury futures roll," we need to understand what a Treasury is — and why the largest, deepest, most-traded market on Earth orbits around it.

Imagine the U.S. government needs money. It wants to build a road, pay soldiers, mail Social Security checks. It collects taxes — but not enough. So it borrows. The way it borrows is by selling IOUs called Treasury securities.

You give the government, say, $1,000 today. In return, it gives you a piece of paper (well, a digital record) promising to pay you back $1,000 in ten years, plus a small interest payment every six months along the way. That's it. That's a Treasury bond.

Treasury
noun · finance

A debt security issued by the U.S. government. There are three flavors based on how long until you get your money back:

• Bills — 4 weeks to 1 year (no interest payments, sold at a discount)

• Notes — 2, 3, 5, 7, 10 years (pay interest twice a year)

• Bonds — 20, 30 years (pay interest twice a year)

The U.S. Treasury market is roughly $27 trillion in size. Every pension fund, central bank, hedge fund, and insurance company on the planet holds some. When people say "the risk-free rate" — Treasuries are what they mean. They're the bedrock.

The single most important fact about bonds

When interest rates go up, bond prices go down. And vice versa. This is the law of gravity in fixed income.

Intuition

You hold a bond paying 3% per year. Suddenly the government issues new bonds paying 5%. Who would buy your 3% bond at full price? Nobody. To sell it, you must discount it — drop the price until its lower coupon, sold at a cheaper price, gives the new buyer a 5% return. So your bond's price falls when rates rise.

DV01 — the most useful number in fixed income

Since prices and rates move inversely, traders need a number that says: "If rates move 1 basis point (0.01%), how many dollars do I gain or lose on this bond?" That number is called DV01 — "Dollar Value of 01 (basis point)."

EQUATION 1.1 — DV01 $$\text{DV01} = -\frac{\Delta P}{\Delta y} \approx \text{Duration} \times P \times 0.0001$$

A 10-year Treasury with $1 million face value might have a DV01 of about $900. Meaning: if yields rise by 1 basis point, you lose $900. If they fall by 1 basis point, you make $900.

Like you're five

DV01 is the sensitivity of your bond's price to tiny moves in interest rates. Longer bonds have bigger DV01s because their cash flows are further away — they're more "stretched out" so any change in rates has a bigger compounded effect. A 30-year bond's DV01 is much larger than a 2-year bond's DV01. Remember this — it matters in Chapter 6.

CHAPTER 02

Futures contracts — promises about the future

A futures contract is a legally binding agreement to buy or sell something at a fixed price, on a specified future date. That's the whole concept. The complexity is in the wrapping.

Let's start with a farming example because it's the cleanest. Imagine you're a corn farmer. It's April. You'll harvest in October. The current corn price is $4/bushel — great. But in October, prices might crash to $2. You'd be ruined.

So you go to the futures exchange and you sell a corn futures contract that says: "I, the farmer, promise to deliver 5,000 bushels of corn on October 15th for $4/bushel." Some food company on the other side buys this contract — they want to lock in their input price too.

October arrives. You harvest. You deliver corn. They give you $4 × 5,000 = $20,000. Done. The market price in October didn't matter — you locked it in months earlier.

From corn to Treasuries

Now replace corn with U.S. Treasury bonds. A Treasury futures contract is a promise to deliver a particular Treasury bond at a particular price on a particular future date. The Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME) runs the market.

There are six main Treasury futures contracts, named by their position on the yield curve:

TickerWhat it tracksNotionalUsed to hedge
TU2-year Treasury notes$200,000Short-end rate risk
FV5-year Treasury notes$100,000Belly of the curve
TY10-year Treasury notes$100,000The benchmark
UXYUltra 10-year notes$100,000Longer 10s
USClassic Treasury bonds (~20yr)$100,000Long end
WNUltra-long bonds (25-30yr)$100,000Duration risk

Each contract has four delivery months per year: March (H), June (M), September (U), December (Z). So "TYH0" means "10-year Treasury future, March 2020 expiry." (That notation is everywhere in the paper — now you can read it.)

Key wrinkle

Unlike corn, a Treasury futures contract doesn't say "deliver bond X." It says "deliver any Treasury bond from this approved list (the deliverable basket)." The short (seller) gets to pick which one. This single design choice is the reason this entire paper exists. We'll unpack it in Chapter 3.

Why futures exist (and why traders love them)

  • Leverage — you only post a small "margin" (maybe 2% of notional), so $1 of capital controls $50 of bonds. Hedge funds love this.
  • Liquidity — futures often trade more volume than the underlying cash bonds (look at Exhibit 2 in the paper — futures volume is 1-3x cash volume!).
  • Standardization — every contract is identical, so anyone can trade with anyone.
  • Hedging — pension funds with bond portfolios can short futures to protect against rising rates.
CHAPTER 03

The cheapest-to-deliver puzzle

Treasury futures let the short (seller) deliver any bond from a basket. Naturally, the short will deliver whichever bond is cheapest. That bond is called the CTD — and tracking it is half the game.

Suppose a 10-year Treasury futures contract says: "On delivery day, the short delivers a Treasury with at least 6.5 years and at most 10 years remaining to maturity." That's not one bond — that's a list of maybe 8-15 bonds, each with different coupons and prices.

The conversion factor

Now here's a problem: a 6.5-year bond with a 1% coupon and a 9.5-year bond with a 4% coupon are very different. The exchange needs to make them "comparable." So they use a conversion factor (CF) — a number, slightly above or below 1, that adjusts the invoice price.

EQUATION 3.1 — Invoice price the short receives $$\text{Invoice} = \text{Futures Price} \times \text{CF} + \text{Accrued Interest}$$

The conversion factor is calculated as the price the bond would have if its yield were exactly 6% (a historical convention from when 6% was the "normal" yield). It tries to neutralize differences across the basket — but it doesn't do it perfectly.

The CTD calculation

The short wants to maximize profit. They will buy whichever bond gives the smallest "gross basis":

EQUATION 3.2 — Gross basis $$\text{Gross Basis} = \text{Bond Price} - (\text{Futures Price} \times \text{CF})$$

The bond with the lowest gross basis is the cheapest-to-deliver (CTD). The market collectively figures out which bond this is, and the futures price ends up pricing off that specific bond, not the average of the basket.

Figure 3.1. Each bar represents one bond's gross basis. The short picks the lowest bar — that's the CTD. The futures market essentially prices off the CTD bond, not some average. When the CTD switches from one bond to another (due to yield changes), things get interesting.
Intuition

Think of it like a coupon-redemption rule: the futures contract is "redeem for one Treasury, any flavor from the basket." The short hunts for the bargain. That bargain — the CTD — is usually the bond with the highest yield relative to its conversion-factor-adjusted price.

The paper's footnote 3 mentions that "a generic consequence of reference yields at 6% is a very highly determined and stable CTD issue." Translation: when actual yields are far from 6% (as they were in 2020 at ~1%), one specific bond dominates the basket and is the CTD virtually all the time. When yields are near 6%, the CTD can switch between bonds frequently, which is exciting but rare today.

CHAPTER 04

The basis — the secret price hiding in plain sight

"Basis" is the gap between the cash bond and the futures contract. Trading this gap is a multi-trillion-dollar industry. And in March 2020, it broke.

You now know that futures price tracks the CTD. But the CTD's actual cash market price and its price-implied-by-futures aren't quite the same. Why? Because owning the cash bond gives you interest payments (carry), but owning the future doesn't. There's also financing cost — if you buy the bond, you have to fund it.

The basis trade — how hedge funds make money

Here's the classic cash-futures basis trade, executed by leveraged funds:

  1. Buy the CTD cash bond (pay $100).
  2. Short the futures contract (locks in your sell price).
  3. Finance the bond purchase in the repo market (borrow $100 overnight using the bond as collateral).
  4. Earn the bond's coupon. Pay repo interest. Pocket the difference.
  5. At delivery, hand over the bond to satisfy the short futures position.

The profit you lock in is approximately the net basis:

EQUATION 4.1 — Net basis (carry trade profit) $$\text{Net Basis} = \text{Gross Basis} - \text{Carry}$$
EQUATION 4.2 — Carry from holding the bond $$\text{Carry} = \text{Coupon Income} - \text{Repo Financing Cost}$$

If net basis is positive, the trade makes money. Hedge funds pile in, drive the futures price up (or the cash price down) until net basis ≈ 0 and the trade is no longer profitable. This is the equilibrium that gets violated during crises.

Repo
noun · short for "repurchase agreement"

A way to borrow short-term money using bonds as collateral. You sell a bond today with an agreement to buy it back tomorrow at a slightly higher price. That tiny price difference is the interest rate (the "repo rate"). It's how virtually all bond positions are financed.

When the paper talks about "1M repo" or "GC/OIS," it means: short-term financing rates that determine the cost of funding bond inventory.

Why this trade is "convergence"

On delivery day, the futures contract literally becomes the bond. So the futures price and the cash price must converge to (nearly) the same number. The basis trade is a bet that this convergence will happen on schedule. It almost always does — except when it doesn't.

Figure 4.1. A schematic basis trade. The cash bond is bought above the CF-adjusted futures price; the gap (basis) erodes as delivery approaches. Profit equals the area between the lines, less financing.
The March 2020 break

In March 2020, COVID panic hit. Investors dumped Treasuries (yes — even Treasuries) to raise cash. Cash bond prices fell relative to futures, causing the basis to widen instead of converge. Hedge funds running this trade lost billions overnight. They unwound positions en masse, which is exactly what the paper means by "the most substantial pullback in Treasury futures open interest on record." The "cleanout in positioning among the leveraged fund community" the abstract mentions is this exact event.

CHAPTER 05

Why futures expire, and what "the roll" actually is

Futures contracts have expiration dates. Anyone with a position must either close it or actually deliver/take bonds. Most people don't want to deliver. So they "roll" — close the expiring contract and open the next one.

Picture an asset manager — say, a pension fund — that uses TY (10-year Treasury futures) to maintain duration exposure. They're long 10,000 TY contracts. The current contract is TYU0 (September 2020), expiring in mid-September. They have two choices:

  1. Take delivery of $1 billion worth of Treasury bonds. (Operationally annoying; requires custody, settlement, etc.)
  2. Roll the position: sell 10,000 TYU0 and simultaneously buy 10,000 TYZ0 (December 2020). Now their exposure continues for another 3 months.

Almost everyone picks option 2. This is the calendar roll — and it happens four times a year across all six futures contracts. Trillions of dollars rotate.

Who rolls early, who rolls late

The paper makes a critical distinction. Two tribes:

Real Money (Asset Managers, Reserve Managers)

Roll early. They don't want any risk of accidentally getting delivered. Operationally they can't always take bonds. So they roll about 2 weeks before expiry, regardless of price.

They are net long futures. To roll, they must sell the front contract and buy the back. This selling pressure pushes the front price down relative to back — a "bearish" pressure on the calendar spread.

Leveraged Funds (Hedge Funds, Basis Traders)

Roll late or hold to delivery. They want to capture the last drops of basis convergence. They're tactical and price-sensitive.

They are net short futures (against long cash bonds). They provide the other side of the asset managers' selling. They're a "pricing backstop."

The roll timing

The paper's Exhibit 5 shows that most open interest rotates from front to back between 10 and 4 business days before first delivery. It's a fast transition — a wave of activity in about a week.

The first notice date

This is a specific calendar date. After this date, holders of long futures positions can be assigned a delivery — meaning they'll receive bonds. Most asset managers want their position closed before this date. So everything in the paper is timed relative to this anchor.

CHAPTER 06

The weighted calendar spread — the price of the roll

When you roll, you're effectively trading one spread: front futures price minus back futures price. But there's a wrinkle — the two contracts don't have identical DV01s. So you weight them.

Here is one of the central equations of the paper. Stare at it. Memorize it. Tattoo it somewhere unobtrusive.

EQUATION 6.1 — Weighted calendar spread $$\text{WCS} = P_{\text{front}} - P_{\text{back}} \times \text{HR}$$

Where:

  • $P_{\text{front}}$ = price of the expiring (front) futures contract
  • $P_{\text{back}}$ = price of the next (back) futures contract
  • $\text{HR}$ = hedge ratio = $\frac{\text{DV01}_{\text{front}}}{\text{DV01}_{\text{back}}}$

Why do we weight?

Remember from Chapter 1: DV01 measures sensitivity to rates. The front and back contracts often have different CTDs, with different maturities and therefore different DV01s. If you just compute $P_{\text{front}} - P_{\text{back}}$, you're not isolating "the roll" — you're also picking up directional moves in interest rates.

Multiplying $P_{\text{back}}$ by the hedge ratio scales it so that a parallel shift in rates moves both legs by the same dollar amount. The weighted spread then becomes rate-neutral — it isolates pure roll dynamics.

A worked example

Suppose for TYU0 (front) and TYZ0 (back):

  • $P_{\text{front}} = 139.00$
  • $P_{\text{back}} = 138.50$
  • $\text{DV01}_{\text{front}} = \$82$ per contract
  • $\text{DV01}_{\text{back}} = \$83$ per contract

Then $\text{HR} = 82/83 = 0.9880$.

$\text{WCS} = 139.00 - 138.50 \times 0.9880 = 139.00 - 136.84 = 2.16$

Now suppose rates suddenly fall by 5 basis points overnight. Both contracts rally. Naively, the unweighted spread $P_{\text{front}} - P_{\text{back}}$ would change a tiny bit (because their sensitivities differ). But the weighted spread stays constant — exactly what we want, because rates moving shouldn't affect "the roll price."

Why does the WCS even have a non-zero value?

If futures were just promises to deliver bonds, both contracts (front and back) should be priced identically up to financing differences. The WCS exists because:

  1. The back contract has 3 more months of carry to its delivery date
  2. The back contract has 3 more months of repo financing to pay
  3. The back contract has a different CTD with different yields
  4. The back contract has more optionality (more time for things to happen)

Those four bullets are exactly the four drivers of the roll. We attack them one by one in the next chapter.

There is no consistent driver of the weighted calendar spreads during the roll across these contracts, perhaps with the exception of the realized change in the 1M/4M repo term structure.
CHAPTER 07

The four drivers — repo, positioning, RV, and the wildcard

The paper's intellectual core. Each driver pulls the calendar spread in a different direction. Understanding each one — and how they interact — is the entire toolkit.

Driver 1: Repo rates

This is the most consistent driver across contracts (the paper's Exhibit 7 shows repo curve hatching for almost every contract).

The mechanics

To hold the cash bond against a short futures, you finance it in repo. If repo rates rise, that financing cost rises. Holding the bond becomes more expensive. The carry shrinks. The basis trade becomes less attractive. Futures richen relative to cash (futures price goes up relative to the bond).

Now — the front contract only has ~3 months of remaining financing exposure; the back contract has ~6 months. So when repo rates rise uniformly, the back contract richens more than the front. That means $P_{\text{back}}$ goes up more than $P_{\text{front}}$, so the WCS = $P_{\text{front}} - P_{\text{back}} \times HR$ falls.

EQUATION 7.1 — Paper's rule of thumb $$\Delta \text{WCS} \approx -\frac{\Delta \text{Repo}}{4}$$

So if repo rises by 4 bp uniformly, WCS falls by about 1 tick (32nds of a point in Treasury futures language). The paper says 1 bp of steepening in the 1M/4M repo curve = 1-3 ticks of calendar spread move.

Why "1M/4M"?

1-month repo covers financing for the front contract (close to expiry). 4-month repo covers financing for the back. The spread between them — the "term structure" — captures how the cost of financing differs across the two delivery windows. That spread is the most relevant repo signal.

Driver 2: Positioning

We covered this in Chapter 5. Real money (asset managers) are persistent net longs and roll early. Their forced selling of the front contract pushes the front price down, which means WCS falls when AM net longs are high.

The paper notes (and this is subtle) that the sensitivity of WCS to AM positioning has declined over recent years. Why? Because overall open interest has grown — there's more market depth, so the same volume of forced rolls has less price impact. In a deep pool, a stone makes smaller ripples.

Leveraged fund positioning

This is the counterpart. Lev funds are net short. They hold their shorts late into the cycle, providing the buy-side of the asset managers' rolls. If lev fund shorts collapse (as in March 2020), there's no one to absorb the AM selling pressure — so the roll progresses much faster and WCS gets bearish faster. This is the paper's worry: with lev fund shorts subdued post-March 2020, the cushion has thinned.

Driver 3: Relative value (the CTD micro-curve)

This one's a bit more subtle. The front and back contracts often have different CTD bonds — usually the back's CTD is slightly longer maturity.

For example, the front might have a 6.8-year CTD; the back has a 7.1-year CTD. The "micro-curve" between those two points (6.8 vs 7.1 years) can move independently of the broader Treasury curve. If the 7.1-year cheapens relative to the 6.8-year (i.e. its yield rises by more), then the back CTD bond price falls more, which drags the back futures price down — and WCS rises.

Reading Exhibit 13 of the paper

The paper shows that in 50-60% of roll cycles since 2015, the direction of the front/back CTD price spread aligns with the WCS move. For TY specifically, this jumps to 68%. The takeaway: keep an eye on which bond is becoming the CTD for the back contract, and watch its richness/cheapness vs. the front CTD.

Driver 4: The wildcard option

This is the most exotic driver — and the one the paper devotes the most attention to. Pay close attention.

The mechanics

Treasury futures settle (i.e., the official daily price is set) at 3:00 PM EST. But the futures short can wait until 7:00 PM EST to officially declare intent to deliver. That's a 4-hour window during which:

  • The futures price is already locked in (so the invoice amount is fixed).
  • But the bond market is still trading (especially in Asia hours).
  • If the bond price spikes after 3 PM, the short can buy a cheaper bond earlier in the day, deliver it that night, and pocket the difference.

That free-look option is the wildcard option. It has positive value, especially when:

  • Volatility is high (more likely to get a big post-3pm move)
  • The conversion factor of the CTD is low (so a bond price move translates to bigger profit on the invoice difference)
EQUATION 7.2 — Wildcard exercise condition (paper's footnote 6) $$\text{Gross Basis} \times \frac{\text{CF}}{1 - \text{CF}} < P_{\text{late}} - P_{\text{3pm}}$$

Translation: exercise the wildcard if the post-3pm price rise ($P_{\text{late}} - P_{\text{3pm}}$) exceeds the residual gross basis you'd be giving up.

Why long-end futures have more wildcard value

Long-end futures (US, WN) have CTDs with very low conversion factors (sometimes 0.5-0.7) and bonds with high price volatility. Both make the wildcard valuable. Short-end (TU, FV) have CFs near 1.0 and lower vol — wildcards there are worth almost nothing. This is why the paper says wildcard pricing is "most acute at the long end."

Summary table — the bullish/bearish signs

From the paper's Appendix (page 10), reproduced and explained:

CHAPTER 08

Putting it all together — reading the paper line by line

Now we go back to the paper armed with all the vocabulary. Every cryptic sentence should now click.

The opening paragraph, decoded

"Following the significant bout of volatility in the Treasury/futures basis in March and cleanout in positioning among the leveraged fund community, there is somewhat greater uncertainty around the Treasury futures roll than previous cycles."

Translation: The basis trade blew up in March 2020 (Chapter 4). Hedge funds got margin-called and unwound their shorts. With them gone, the natural buyers of the front contract during the roll period are missing. Therefore the price action during the roll is less predictable.

"Subdued leveraged fund short positioning is likely to be a key theme for long-end contracts near term, with implications for the pricing of the wildcard option."

Translation: Lev funds are usually the ones bidding up the wildcard (because they're short futures and want to monetize the option). With them sidelined, the wildcard might be underpriced. From the table in Chapter 7 — undervalued wildcard means a bearish bias for the WCS.

"While not necessarily predictive, this diagnostic approach is a useful way to rank different factors…"

Translation: The authors regress the realized WCS change on each driver, ex-post. They're not claiming you can predict the WCS from these variables in advance. They're just saying "looking back, here's what mattered most." This is honest empiricism — they don't oversell it.

"For a parallel shift in repo rates… the calendar spread change is dominated by the move in the back contract relative to the front."

Translation: Equation 7.1 in our tutorial. The back has more remaining carry exposure, so it's more sensitive to repo. Higher repo → back richens more → WCS falls.

"Higher financing rates imply a higher cost of funding the cash bond position…"

Translation: Equation 4.2. If repo goes up, the holder of cash bonds is paying more to finance. The trade's carry shrinks. To compensate, futures must richen (or cash cheapen). Either way, gross basis narrows.

"…the magnitude of this sensitivity has declined over the past few years."

Translation: The market is bigger and deeper. Same forced rolling = smaller price impact. The asset manager positioning signal has lost some of its punch as open interest grew.

"…the CTD box spread to OIS (on a forward basis to deliver) is highly mean-reverting during roll periods…"

Translation: The yield spread between back-CTD and front-CTD, both measured against OIS (overnight-indexed swap, a "clean" benchmark rate), tends to oscillate around a stable mean rather than drift. So if you see it stretched far from average, expect a snap-back. This is a tradable signal.

The Dickey-Fuller test (Exhibit 14) is a statistical test for mean-reversion vs. random-walk behavior. Lower p-values = stronger mean reversion. FV has a p-value of 0.7%, very statistically significant.

CHAPTER 09

The March 2020 shock — what actually happened

Why this paper exists. The single biggest stress test the Treasury futures market has faced in modern memory, and the data signatures that followed.

Throughout the 2010s, the cash-futures basis trade was a beautiful machine. Hedge funds bought tens of billions of Treasuries, shorted futures against them, financed in repo, and clipped basis points of carry. The trade was so reliable it scaled enormously — leverage of 50-to-1 or higher.

What broke

In early March 2020, COVID hit globally. Every investor everywhere wanted dollars. They sold whatever they could — including Treasuries, the supposed "safe haven." Cash bond prices fell. Simultaneously, dealers (the intermediaries) hit capacity limits — their balance sheets were already swollen and post-2008 regulations capped further expansion.

The basis widened. Hedge funds running the trade faced margin calls. They had to liquidate cash bonds at fire-sale prices to raise the margin. That further depressed cash bond prices, widening the basis more, triggering more margin calls. Classic doom loop.

The Fed eventually stepped in massively — buying Treasuries directly, expanding repo operations (TOMOs in the paper), and effectively backstopping the dealer system. The crisis abated by late March.

The footprint in the data

The paper's Exhibit 1 shows a record collapse in open interest in March — about 3 million TY-equivalents wiped out in a quarter. Exhibit 3 shows leveraged fund gross shorts crashed by ~50% and stayed depressed.

Figure 9.1. Stylized version of the paper's Exhibit 3. After a decade of growth in basis-trade positioning, March 2020 wiped out roughly half of leveraged fund gross shorts in days. They never fully returned.

Why this matters for the August 2020 roll

The paper was written in August 2020, looking ahead to the September 2020 roll cycle. The thesis: with leveraged funds still licking their wounds, several drivers behave differently than usual.

  • Wildcard pricing — lev funds usually bid the wildcard up via the gross basis. With them absent, the wildcard may be cheap. This is actionable — buy futures shorts to gain wildcard exposure if you think vol will be high.
  • Roll progression — without lev funds as the patient counterparty, asset manager selling has less of a backstop. Expect faster roll, more bearish WCS.
  • Repo — Fed's emergency repo facilities (TOMOs) have suppressed the tail risk of overnight repo spikes (like the September 2019 spike that scared everyone).

A final, philosophical note

The Treasury futures roll is not exciting in the sense of crypto pumps or meme stocks. It is exciting in the sense that billions of dollars hinge on the precise pricing of a derivative wrapped around a contractual option to deliver any one of fifteen government bonds within a six-week window. The actors are large, the timeframes short, the math relentless.

The paper you read isn't trying to predict markets. It's trying to give a structured framework — a vocabulary, really — to anyone who wants to think about the roll like a professional. With this tutorial in hand, you now share that vocabulary. Re-read the paper from page 1. You'll find that what once was opaque is now plain.

The roll is not a single number to be predicted. It is a forest of forces, and the trader's craft is to know which trees are growing.
