Chapter 19 · Pricing Models

Why do we even need a "model"?

A pricing model is not a crystal ball. It's a translator — it takes the prices the market already agrees on for simple contracts, and uses them to infer a fair price for a complicated contract nobody is directly quoting.

Build the intuition

Imagine you know the price of apples and the price of oranges. Someone asks: "what should a fruit basket with 2 apples and 3 oranges cost?" You don't need a new theory of fruit — you just add up what you already know. Vanilla FX options (calls and puts) are the apples and oranges. Their market prices, across every strike and every maturity, are visible on every trading screen. An exotic contract — a barrier, a touch, a digital — is the fruit basket. A pricing model is the arithmetic that turns "what vanillas cost" into "what this basket should cost."

The chain of logic runs left to right, and every arrow is a real, computable step:

Vanilla market quotes (ATM volatility, risk reversals, butterflies — don't worry, we'll define these) get assembled into a volatility smile at one maturity, and smiles across every maturity stack into a full volatility surface. That surface does two things simultaneously: it directly reprices every vanilla option, and it becomes the calibration target for an exotic pricing model. The model, once calibrated, can price contracts that have no direct market quote at all — a knock-out barrier, a touch, a digital.

The one rule that governs this entire chapter

More features in a model does not automatically mean a better price. Every extra parameter (stochastic volatility, jumps, stochastic rates) has to be calibrated, and calibration takes time. A five-hour price is useless to a trader who needs to quote a client in five seconds, or to a risk manager who needs to revalue an entire book before the market gaps. So traders pick the simplest model whose dynamic matches the contract's real risk — nothing more.

Exotic models split into two families, and it's worth knowing which family a model belongs to before you ever look at its formula:

Smile models

Calibrated to the volatility surface. They answer: "given how the market prices risk at every strike, what's the fair price of this exotic?" Interest rates are usually treated as fixed/deterministic. Includes: stochastic volatility, local volatility, mixed volatility, jump diffusion.

Interest rate models

Let interest rates move randomly, and even correlate with spot. They matter most for long-dated contracts (roughly 2+ years) where a decade of possible rate paths meaningfully changes the value of a payoff. Volatility of the underlying is usually treated as fixed/deterministic.

Some models combine both — but remember the rule above. We'll walk through each smile model in turn, because they're the ones you'll touch every single day on a trading desk, and each one has a distinct, learnable "personality."

19.1 · Smile Models

Stochastic volatility: let volatility have a life of its own

Black-Scholes assumes volatility is one fixed number for the life of the option. In reality, volatility itself jitters around randomly — sometimes calm, sometimes violent. Stochastic volatility models give volatility its own random process, correlated with spot.

The most famous of these is the Heston model (1993). It looks intimidating in equation form, but it's really just two coupled random walks: one for spot, one for variance (volatility squared).

Heston stochastic differential equations
$$\frac{dS_t}{S_t} = (r_{CCY2}-r_{CCY1})\,dt + \sqrt{v_t}\,dW_{1,t}$$ $$dv_t = \lambda(\alpha - v_t)\,dt + \beta\sqrt{v_t}\left(\rho\, dW_{1,t} + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\, dW_{2,t}\right)$$

α — long-run variance

Where variance drifts back to over time. Think of it as "gravity" pulling volatility toward its long-term average.

λ — mean reversion speed

How hard that gravity pulls. High λ snaps volatility back quickly; low λ lets it wander.

β — vol-of-vol

How jittery volatility itself is. This is the parameter that controls the wings of the smile.

...and ρ (correlation) ties the two random walks together — it's the parameter that controls the tilt (skew) of the smile.

Build the intuition

Picture a coin that itself has a wobble in how biased it is. Most of the time it's a fair coin (50/50), but sometimes it drifts toward "a bit biased to heads," and sometimes toward "a bit biased to tails," and how far it drifts is random too. Over many flips, that wobble makes extreme outcomes (way more heads than expected, or way more tails) more likely than a plain fair coin would ever produce. That's exactly what vol-of-vol does to spot: it fattens the tails of where spot can end up, which is precisely what a volatility smile is describing — options far from today's price being worth more than flat (constant-volatility) Black-Scholes would say.

Notice something the textbook explicitly flags: when you raise β from zero, both wings rise, and so does the ATM level — unlike a typical hand-drawn smile where the ATM point usually stays anchored. That's a real quirk of Heston's dynamics, not a drawing error. And when you raise ρ, the smile stops being a symmetric smile and starts tilting: one side rises relative to the other, which is exactly what a market risk reversal looks like.

Where the model runs into trouble

To fit the market's smile well, Heston (and stochastic vol models generally) typically need more volatility-of-implied-volatility than is actually observed. Traders call this "overvaluing volatility convexity." The practical symptom: prices on contracts with heavy convexity exposure — like double-no-touch options — often come out too high versus the real interbank broker market. The skew can also get swamped by mean reversion, and at short maturities some stochastic vol models simply can't generate enough vol-of-vol to match the smile at all.

Deeper: why is it called "sticky delta"?

Pure stochastic volatility models don't depend on the level of spot at all — only on the randomness layered on top of it. That means if spot moves, the model regenerates the same shaped smile centered on the new spot (in delta space). Traders call this sticky delta: the volatility quoted for "the 25-delta call" stays roughly the same even as spot drifts, because the smile is anchored to moneyness (delta), not to a fixed price level. Compare this to local volatility below, which behaves almost oppositely.

19.2 · Smile Models

Local volatility: one deterministic surface, no randomness in vol itself

Developed by Bruno Dupire in the mid-1990s. Instead of giving volatility its own random process, local volatility makes it a fixed — but spot- and time-dependent — function: σ(S,t).

Local volatility SDE
$$\frac{dS_t}{S_t} = (r_{CCY2}-r_{CCY1})\,dt + \sigma(S_t,t)\,dW_t$$
Build the intuition

Think of local volatility as a terrain map, not a weather forecast. At every possible (spot level, future date) coordinate, there's one fixed number for how bumpy the road is there — no randomness in the bumpiness itself, only in which road spot happens to travel. Dupire's insight was that if you know the entire vanilla volatility surface, there's a unique such terrain map that reproduces it exactly, with zero free parameters to guess.

Because there's nothing to calibrate — the terrain map is solved for directly from market vanilla prices — local volatility is fast to set up and gives an exact fit to the vanilla surface, no exceptions. That's a big practical advantage.

Compare implied vol vs. the local vol it implies

Local volatility is always steeper than implied volatility — roughly twice the slope, a mathematical consequence of Dupire's formula. Toggle between a pair with a small downside skew (USD/JPY-like) and one with a large topside skew (USD/BRL-like):

Implied volatility (what vanillas trade at) Local volatility (the terrain map)
Where the model runs into trouble

The opposite problem to Heston: because there's no actual randomness in volatility, local vol undervalues volatility convexity. Double-no-touch prices tend to come out too low. Forward smiles are undervalued too (query the model for the 1yr-in-1yr forward smile and you'll typically see much flatter wings and skew than today's 1yr smile shows — a real, checkable diagnostic). And because volatility is a fixed function of time and spot with no randomness of its own, local vol should never be used to price forward volatility agreements or forward-start options, whose entire value comes from the randomness of future implied volatility.

A free diagnostic tool

If the vanilla volatility surface you feed into Dupire's formula contains an arbitrage — a call spread priced with the wrong sign, or a calendar spread that's cheaper further out — the local volatility surface becomes undefined (you'd need to take a square root of a negative number). In practice, traders exploit this: if your local vol model blows up, that's a red flag your vanilla surface itself has an arbitrage in it.

19.3 · Smile Models

Mixed volatility: splitting the difference on purpose

Stochastic vol overvalues convexity. Local vol undervalues it. So what does almost every FX derivatives desk on the planet actually trade off of? A blend of the two — a "stochastic local volatility" model.

One way to blend them — a Heston model with a local-vol component and a mixing weight ξ
$$\frac{dS_t}{S_t} = (r_{CCY2}-r_{CCY1})\,dt + \sqrt{v_t}\,\hat\sigma(S_t,t)\,dW_{1,t}$$ $$dv_t = \lambda(t)(\alpha - v_t)\,dt + \xi(t)\beta(t)\sqrt{v_t}\left(\rho(t)\,dW_{1,t}+\sqrt{1-\rho(t)^2}\,dW_{2,t}\right)$$

A mixing weight of 0% is pure local volatility. A mixing weight of 100% is pure stochastic volatility. Everything in between interpolates — and traders set that weight (per tenor, even) to match whatever the interbank exotics broker market is actually paying for convexity.

Why this one model dominates the industry

Variations of mixed volatility models are used by the vast majority of FX derivatives desks to price exotics out to about two years, in most currency pairs. It's so effective that development of brand-new single-asset pricing models has largely stopped — the industry converged. For obscure currency pairs with too few interbank quotes to calibrate a mixing weight directly, traders borrow the mixing weight from a similar, more liquid pair.

19.4 · Smile Models

Jump diffusion: when spot doesn't glide, it teleports

Every model so far assumes spot moves continuously — no matter how small a time slice you look at, spot only creeps. Real markets sometimes gap: a central bank decision, a currency peg breaking, a surprise headline. The Merton model (1976) adds sudden, discontinuous jumps on top of ordinary diffusion.

Merton jump diffusion SDE
$$\frac{dS_t}{S_t} = (r_{CCY2}-r_{CCY1}-\lambda\kappa)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t + (y_t-1)\,dN_t$$

Here N_t is a Poisson process — it fires jump events at average rate λ per year — and each jump multiplies spot by a log-normally distributed factor y_t with mean κ. Notice the drift term picks up a -λκ correction: since jumps add extra randomness, the steady drift has to be adjusted to compensate, or the model's average path would drift to the wrong place.

Merton is popular for a subtle reason beyond realism: it has a semi-closed-form price for vanilla options, so — just like Heston — it can be calibrated fast. And like Heston, its parameters map intuitively onto smile shape: jump frequency and jump size distribution directly sculpt the wings and skew.

Where it actually gets used

Jump models don't consistently match interbank exotics prices in liquid, freely-floating currency pairs — real markets there don't behave like clean Poisson jumps. But in managed or pegged currency pairs, where spot genuinely sits still for long stretches and then jumps discontinuously when a peg adjusts or breaks, jump diffusion's dynamic is a much better match to reality. It's also used as a diagnostic: back out the jump probability and size the market's smile implies, and compare it against your own intuition about devaluation risk.

19.5 · Interest Rate Models

Stochastic interest rates: the other source of randomness

Every model above quietly assumed interest rates in both currencies were fixed numbers. In reality, rates wiggle too — and on long-dated contracts, that wiggle matters.

Adding random "short rate" processes to both currencies
$$\frac{dS_t}{S_t}=(r_{CCY2,t}-r_{CCY1,t})\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t$$ $$dr_{CCY1,t}=a_1(t)\,dt+b_1\,dW_{1,t} \qquad dr_{CCY2,t}=a_2(t)\,dt+b_2\,dW_{2,t}$$

When it matters

Mostly on contracts beyond roughly two years to maturity, or on any contract with a large explicit sensitivity to rates. Below that horizon, the extra complexity usually isn't worth the calibration cost — remember the rule from the top of this chapter.

The trap to avoid

You cannot just price a deal with a smile model, price it again with a rate model, and add the two adjustments together. Smile and interest rates genuinely interact — which is why local volatility is often layered inside a stochastic rate model, since it adds smile realism with zero extra parameters to calibrate.

Chapter 20 · Product Classification

The exotics family tree

Exotic products are grouped by "generation" — roughly, how long they've existed in the market. But here's the counter-intuitive part the textbook is explicit about:

The rule that surprises everyone new to this desk

Higher-generation exotics are often easier to risk manage than first-generation ones, not harder. Features like averaging, accruing notionals, or knockout targets often reduce risk, because they smooth out the very discontinuities that make first-generation products dangerous. Conceptually simple products (a digital, a barrier) are frequently the hardest to actually trade near expiry.

1st generation

The building blocks

European digitals, touch options (one-touch / no-touch), European barriers, American barriers, American vanillas, quanto options. Everything else on this desk is assembled from these pieces.

2nd generation

Extending forwards & vanillas

Window barriers, accrual options, target redemption forwards (TARFs), Asian (average rate) options, compound options, forward-start options.

3rd generation

Multi-asset & volatility products

Basket options, best-of/worst-of, dual digitals, volatility & variance swaps, correlation swaps, forward volatility agreements. Usually risk-managed on separate books.

Chapters 21–23 of this guide (below) live entirely inside the first-generation family, because that's where the foundational intuition — payoff replication, intrinsic value, delta bleed, pin risk — gets built. Once you have that intuition, the higher generations are mostly clever recombinations of it.

20.1 · American Barrier Options

The barrier zoo: it's all just geometry

Every American barrier option's name — regular, reverse, ITM, double — is fully determined by one simple question: where does the barrier sit relative to the strike, and relative to where spot starts?

Build the intuition

A vanilla call only has value once spot is above the strike — that's its "in-the-money" side. A barrier sitting on the other side (out-of-the-money) just chops off the tail of the distribution before it can become valuable: cheaper option, simple story — a "regular" knock-out. A barrier sitting on the same side (in-the-money) is more dangerous: it can wipe out value that's already accrued — a "reverse" knock-out. Push the in-the-money barrier so close that spot starts already past it, and you get the special, extra-dangerous "ITM barrier" case, where the strike itself can never be touched again.

If you're long a barrier (you want it to trigger), that position naturally generates a stop-loss order in the underlying when hedging. This single sentence explains almost all of the delta-hedging behavior you'll see later in the touch options section.

20.2 · Beyond first generation

A field guide to the rest of the menu

You won't build pricing intuition for all of these today — but you should be able to recognize them on a term sheet. Each one is a specific twist on a forward or a vanilla payoff.

2nd gen

Window barrier

A barrier that's only "live" for part of the option's life — front window (starts at inception) or rear window (starts later, runs to expiry).

2nd gen

Accrual option

Notional builds up over time based on daily fixes landing inside a range. The accrual forward is the most common variant.

2nd gen

Target redemption (TARF)

A strip of forwards that knocks out once cumulative client profit hits a target. Very common structured product with corporates.

2nd gen

Asian (average rate) option

Spot-at-maturity and/or the strike gets replaced by an average of fixings — smooths out single-day pin risk entirely.

2nd gen

Compound option

An option on an option — pay a premium now for the right to buy/sell a vanilla later.

2nd gen

Forward-start option

Strike (or barrier) gets set later, from a future spot fix — not known at trade inception.

3rd gen

Basket / best-of / worst-of / dual digital

Multi-currency-pair payoffs whose value depends heavily on correlation between the pairs — not just each pair's own smile.

3rd gen

Vol / variance / correlation swaps, FVAs

Pure bets on realized or forward-implied volatility and correlation themselves, rather than on spot direction.

Chapter 21 · European Digital Options

The simplest payoff that causes the most trouble

A European digital pays a fixed lump of cash if spot finishes above (call) or below (put) a level at maturity — nothing in between. It's the cleanest possible bet: heads you get €1,000,000, tails you get zero.

Payoff at maturity — 100.00 USD/JPY digital call

Priced as a % of the payout notional — e.g. a quote of 23.5% / 24.5% on a USD1m payout means the buyer pays USD 245,000 for a shot at USD 1,000,000. Around 50% roughly means "even money" — the forward sits close to the digital level.

The single hardest sentence in this chapter

The payoff jumps discontinuously across a single pip. Every other Greek exposure in this guide is a smooth curve — this one has a genuine cliff edge, and that cliff edge is the source of everything difficult about trading digitals near expiry.

21.1 · Replication

Building a cliff out of two ramps

You can manufacture a digital's step-function payoff by buying one vanilla call and selling another, very close by, in a large enough notional. As the two strikes squeeze together, the ramp between them gets steeper and steeper — approaching a true vertical cliff in the limit.

X = payout, K₁ = long strike, K₂ = short strike. Tighter strikes ⇒ larger notional needed, because you're squeezing the same total payout into a narrower price range.

If instead the payout currency is CCY1, the same idea applies but the strikes must be flipped into reciprocal terms first (because a CCY1 payout naturally comes from a CCY2 notional) — the notionals per leg end up asymmetric even though the strikes look symmetric in price space.

Why this matters beyond pricing

This replication isn't just theoretical. Traders literally hedge digital risk with real, tradeable vanilla call/put spreads — wide ones when there's plenty of time to expiry (the hedge stays relevant to roughly the 25-delta strike level, about one month out), tighter ones as expiry approaches and the true digital risk needs to be matched more precisely.

21.2 · Pricing

A live Black-Scholes digital pricer

Under Black-Scholes, a digital's price is a direct readout of the cumulative normal distribution — literally, "the risk-neutral probability that spot finishes past the level," discounted back to today.

Deeper: why is there a link to vanilla delta?

Under Black-Scholes, a CCY1%-payout digital's price equals exactly the delta of a vanilla option struck at the same level, to the same expiry. Take one more derivative with respect to spot, and the digital's delta equals that vanilla's gamma. This is a genuinely useful mental shortcut for traders sizing digital risk on the fly — but it's a Black-Scholes-only relationship; it breaks once the smile is included, which is exactly why real desks price digitals via smile-aware replication (Section 21.1) rather than the flat-vol formula above.

Traders don't stop at the flat theoretical value (TV) above — the real, tradeable price sits on top of it as a TV adjustment, driven by the slope of the volatility smile at the digital level. A useful mental script: "It's a digital call, so the payoff favors topside. That makes me effectively long vega to the downside. If the smile has a downside skew, I'd expect this to trade above flat-vol TV."

21.3 · Gamma & Pin Risk

The last day: half the payout, all at once

As expiry approaches, a digital's gamma (and theta) explodes near the digital level — the curve is racing to become a vertical cliff in one trading day.

Try it — the digital's shape collapses into a cliff as time runs out
Pin risk, in one number

If spot sits near the digital level one day before expiry, the digital's value is roughly 50%. At expiry it becomes either 0% or 100%. That means the maximum possible one-day theta, ignoring any hedge, is half the total payout — arriving all in a single trading session. This is exactly why, on the expiry date itself, traders concentrate on reducing worst-case final-day P&L rather than trying to track smooth Greeks that no longer meaningfully exist.

Which side of the digital spot sits on going into that last day matters enormously — and asymmetrically:

Spot on the winning (positive P&L) side

Theta is earned every day — you start with positive carry, and only lose it if spot moves against you. This is the easier position to risk-manage: your bad outcome is "give back some of what you've already banked."

Spot on the losing (negative P&L) side

Theta is paid every day, but there's a lottery-ticket-style gain if spot crosses. This is the harder position — you start behind, and experienced traders really have been known to shout at a screen, willing the spot rate to move.

Chapter 22 · European Barrier Options

A vanilla with a maturity-only trapdoor

A European barrier only checks the barrier level at maturity — never in between. That's the whole difference from the American barriers in the next chapter, and it makes European barriers dramatically simpler to reason about: they're a pure function of one number, spot at maturity.

Try it — knock-out vs. knock-in payoff, and how it's replicated
Barrier option payoff Plain vanilla (for comparison)
The replication trick, spelled out

A long European knock-out call = (long call spread struck at K and the barrier B) + (short European digital at B). The digital exactly cancels the payoff beyond the barrier, chopping the ramp into a flat line. And since a digital can itself be replicated with vanilla spreads, a European barrier can theoretically be built entirely out of plain vanilla options — no exotic pricing model strictly required, though in practice a smile model or tight replication is used for speed and accuracy.

One more identity worth internalizing, because it will save you from ever needing to memorize a separate knock-in formula: long knock-in + long knock-out = long vanilla, always, for identical contract terms. The digitals embedded in each replication are equal and opposite, and they cancel.

22.1 · Intrinsic Value

Intrinsic value: the size of the cliff, in real money

"Intrinsic value" (also called parity, cliff, or spike) measures the payoff right at the barrier — in other words, exactly how much P&L jumps if spot ends up a single pip on one side of the barrier versus the other at maturity. It's the size of the embedded digital.

Intrinsic value of a European barrier
$$IV_{CCY2} = N_{CCY1}\times|B-K| \qquad\qquad IV_{CCY1\%} = \frac{|B-K|}{B}$$

Why traders reach for this number constantly

If your digital bid-offer runs 2%, expect the barrier's bid-offer to run roughly 2% × intrinsic value.

Chapter 23 · Touch Options

American digitals: does spot ever touch, at any moment at all?

A one-touch pays a fixed cash amount if spot ever trades through a barrier at any time before expiry — not just at the very end. A no-touch pays if it never does. These are the American-barrier cousins of the European digitals from Chapter 21.

One-touch (OT)

Pays out if spot ever trades through the barrier before expiry. Quoted, like a digital, as a % of the payout — because there's no strike to switch currencies against.

No-touch (NT)

Pays out only if spot never trades through the barrier. Its Greeks and TV adjustment are the exact mirror image (equal and opposite) of the matching one-touch.

The everyday-language definition of "touch probability"

Ignoring hedges and discounting, a one-touch's theoretical value is, intuitively, the model's estimate of the probability that spot ever reaches the barrier before expiry. That's not a metaphor — it's literally what the Monte Carlo lab below measures directly, and you can watch it converge to the model's number in real time.

Closed-form probability that GBM ever reaches a barrier before T (reflection principle)
$$P(\text{touch})=N\!\left(\frac{mT-b}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\right)+e^{\frac{2mb}{\sigma^2}}\,N\!\left(\frac{-mT-b}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\right)$$

where b = ln(B/S) and m = (r_d - r_f) - σ²/2 is the drift of log-spot. This is exactly the mathematics behind one-touch theoretical value — a full desk pricer additionally layers in the smile and stochastic-rate adjustments from Chapter 19.

A genuinely surprising result, hiding in these sliders

Push CCY1 rates far above CCY2 rates (very negative swap points) with a downside barrier, and a long one-touch can develop negative vega — more volatility makes it less likely to trigger. Why: with a strongly negative forward drift, the model already expects the forward path to sail straight through the barrier with high probability. Adding volatility lets spot diverge away from that path just as easily as toward it — and since it was already expected to hit, extra randomness can only reduce the chance. Remember: zero volatility never means spot is static — it means spot exactly follows the forward.

23.2 · Greeks Through Time

Delta, vega, and gamma all migrate toward the barrier

A one-touch's Greeks don't sit still — as expiry approaches, every exposure concentrates more and more tightly around the barrier level, then vanishes entirely the instant the option either triggers or expires.

Try it — scrub through time and watch delta, vega, and gamma reshape

Delta

Long into a topside barrier, short into a downside one — value rises as spot nears the barrier. Far from the barrier, delta bleeds away from it over time; close to it, delta bleeds toward it. On the expiry date itself the option becomes pure cash-or-nothing with zero delta — producing a large, sudden delta bleed on the final day.

Vega

Generally long vega (more volatility → more chance of touching), peaking where the touch is most "in the balance" — around the 30% TV point — and that peak marches toward the barrier as time passes. It's exactly zero through the barrier itself, since the contract has already locked in its payout there.

Gamma

Explodes near the barrier into the final days — mirroring the digital's pin risk from Chapter 21. The standard hedge: sell vanillas at (or just in front of) the barrier, to the same expiry, to offset the theta and gamma spike on the last few days.

The delta gap ("stop-loss order in disguise")

The instant a barrier actually knocks, the option's delta jumps discontinuously to zero. A long topside one-touch's delta jumps from "long" to "flat" the moment it triggers — which means the overall trading book suddenly gets shorter delta and needs spot bought right as spot is rallying through the level. That's a stop-loss order, generated automatically by being long the barrier. G10 desks pre-place these delta-gap orders in an order-management system so the net position doesn't jump when the barrier trips.

23.3 · Double-No-Touch

Ranges: betting that nothing dramatic happens

A double-no-touch (DNT), or "range," pays out if spot stays between two barriers for the entire life of the option. Market convention trades single barriers as one-touches, but double barriers as no-touches — so DNTs, not "double one-touches," are the standard interbank product.

Why this is the classic "volga" trade

Inside the range: lower volatility means a better chance spot stays put and the payout is earned — so a long DNT is short vega there. Outside the range: higher volatility means a better chance spot wanders back inside — so it's long vega there. That "short in the middle, long in the wings" shape is exactly a long volga (long convexity-of-volatility) position — which is precisely why double-no-touch prices are the classic diagnostic for whether a model over- or under-values volatility convexity (recall Heston overvalues it; local vol undervalues it — Chapter 19).

Why does more model complexity not mean a better price?+
Calibration takes time, and speed matters for both client quoting and portfolio risk management. Desks pick the simplest model whose dynamic matches the contract's real risk features.
What does vol-of-vol (β) control in Heston? What does correlation (ρ) control?+
β controls the wings (convexity) of the smile. ρ controls the tilt/skew of the smile.
Which smile model overvalues volatility convexity, and which undervalues it?+
Stochastic volatility (e.g. Heston) overvalues it. Local volatility undervalues it. Mixed volatility blends the two to match market prices, especially on convex products like double-no-touch.
What does it mean if a local volatility surface becomes undefined?+
It signals the underlying vanilla volatility surface itself likely contains an arbitrage — a strike-spread or calendar-spread violation.
Why don't jump diffusion models work well in liquid, freely-floating pairs?+
Real liquid markets don't move in clean discontinuous Poisson jumps. Jump models fit best in managed or pegged pairs, where spot genuinely sits still and then gaps.
What is "intrinsic value" on a European barrier option?+
The payoff exactly at the barrier level — the size of the embedded digital, and the main driver of the barrier's bid-offer spread.
Long knock-in + long knock-out = ?+
A long vanilla option with the same strike and expiry — the embedded digitals from each replication cancel exactly.
What's the maximum one-day theta on a European digital, and when does it occur?+
Half the total payout, occurring on the very last trading day if spot sits near the digital level going into expiry.
Being long a barrier generates what kind of hedge order? Being short?+
Long a barrier → a stop-loss order (buy as it rallies through / sell as it falls through). Short a barrier → a take-profit order.
Why can a one-touch have negative vega?+
In high interest-rate-differential pairs with a barrier the forward is already expected to reach, extra volatility lets spot diverge away from that path just as easily as toward it, reducing the touch probability.
What shape is a double-no-touch's vega, and what Greek does that imply exposure to?+
Short vega inside the range, long vega in the wings — a long volga (convexity of volatility) position.
Why are higher-generation exotics sometimes easier to risk manage than first-generation ones?+
Features like averaging, accrual, or knockout targets smooth out the discontinuities (pin risk, gamma spikes) that make conceptually simpler first-generation products so dangerous near expiry.
