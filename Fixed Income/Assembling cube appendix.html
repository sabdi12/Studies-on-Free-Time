The engine that everything rides on

Two of the four skips (cap stripping and the final premium) call the same little engine over and over: the Black formula for the price of an option. You cannot follow the cap-stripping table without it, so we build it first, from the ground, assuming you've never met it.

An interest-rate caplet is a tiny insurance policy: "if the rate $F$ ends up above a strike $K$, pay me the difference; otherwise pay me nothing." Its payoff is $\max(F-K,\,0)$. The fair price today is the average of that payoff over every future the rate might take. Black's 1976 model assumes the rate's future is lognormal (it can drift up or down by percentages, never below zero), and under that one assumption the average has a closed form:

BLACK '76 $$ C = F\,N(d_1) - K\,N(d_2),\qquad d_1=\frac{\ln(F/K)+\tfrac12\sigma^2T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}},\qquad d_2=d_1-\sigma\sqrt{T} $$
Every symbol, named
  • $F$ — the forward rate: the market's best guess today of what the rate will be. Our examples use $F=3\%=0.03$.
  • $K$ — the strike: the level in the contract. "At the money" (ATM) means $K=F$.
  • $\sigma$ — the volatility: how much the rate wobbles, per year. This is the one number the whole cube exists to produce.
  • $T$ — time to expiry in years.
  • $N(\cdot)$ — the standard normal CDF: $N(x)$ is "the probability a standard bell-curve draw lands below $x$." $N(0)=0.5$ (half the bell is left of centre); $N(\infty)=1$; $N(-\infty)=0$. It has no elementary formula, so code approximates it — but you only ever need to look it up.
◈ How to read the formula (not just compute it)

$F\,N(d_1)$ is roughly "the rate, weighted by how likely we are to collect." $K\,N(d_2)$ is "the strike we'll pay, weighted by how likely we pay it." The price is the gap. $d_1$ and $d_2$ are just $F$ and $K$ translated onto the bell curve's ruler (how many standard deviations from the money are we?). When $K=F$ (ATM) and there's no drift, $d_1$ and $d_2$ sit symmetrically around zero — you'll see exactly that in the next chapter.

Skip #1

Pricing one caplet, digit by digit

The original guide's stripping table opens with the 1-year cap priced at 0.003577 and never shows where that came from. A 1-year annual cap is just one caplet, so this is the cleanest possible Black calculation. Forward and strike both 3% (ATM), one year out, vol 30%, discount factor 1.

Black, plugged in — F=K=3%, σ=30%, T=1
First the "total volatility" over the life of the option, $v=\sigma\sqrt{T}$:
$$ v = 0.30\times\sqrt{1} = 0.30 $$
Vol is quoted per year; over $T$ years uncertainty grows like $\sqrt{T}$ (variances add, so std-devs add in quadrature). Here $T=1$, so $v=\sigma$.
Now $d_1$. Since $K=F$, the log term $\ln(F/K)=\ln(1)=0$ — it vanishes, leaving only the drift-correction $\tfrac12 v^2$:
$$ d_1=\frac{0+\tfrac12(0.30)^2}{0.30}=\frac{0.045}{0.30}=0.15 $$
That $\tfrac12\sigma^2$ is the famous lognormal "convexity" nudge — it's why an ATM option isn't perfectly symmetric.
And $d_2$ is just $d_1$ minus the total vol:
$$ d_2 = 0.15 - 0.30 = -0.15 $$
$d_1$ and $d_2$ straddle zero symmetrically ($+0.15$ and $-0.15$) — the signature of an ATM, zero-drift option.
Look up the normal CDF at each (this is the only "table lookup"; code uses the Abramowitz–Stegun approximation):
$$ N(0.15)=0.559618,\qquad N(-0.15)=0.440382 $$
Notice they sum to 1: being 0.15 above centre is exactly as likely as being 0.15 below. $N(0.15)-0.5=0.0596$ is "the little extra above the coin-flip."
Assemble:
$$ C = 0.03\times0.559618 - 0.03\times0.440382 = 0.03\,(0.559618-0.440382) = 0.03\times0.119236 $$ $$ \boxed{\,C = 0.00357706\,} $$
Rounded to six places that's 0.003577 — the first number in the stripping table, no longer a mystery.
arithmetic · the same five lines as a calculator would v = 0.30 * sqrt(1) → 0.300000 d1 = (0 + 0.5*0.30^2) / 0.30 → 0.045 / 0.30 = 0.150000 d2 = 0.15 - 0.30 → -0.150000 N(d1)= 0.559618 , N(d2) = 0.440382 C = 0.03*0.559618 - 0.03*0.440382 = 0.00357706

Skip #2

Cap stripping: the whole table, built and solved

Now the big one. The table in the main guide has five rows of finished numbers. Underneath each row are two things it never shows: how the cap price is assembled from individual caplets, and how the stripped vol is solved for. We do both.

The idea in one sentence

A cap is a bundle of caplets — one for each reset date. A 3-year annual cap = caplet at year 1 + caplet at year 2 + caplet at year 3. Crucially, when the market quotes a single "3-year cap vol" of 26%, it means "price all three caplets at 26%." So:

CAP = SUM OF CAPLETS $$ \text{Cap}(T_n) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} \text{caplet}\big(t_i,\ \sigma_n^{\text{term}}\big) $$

Step A — assemble each cap price

Each cap is priced at its own flat term vol (the market quote). The 2-year cap prices both its caplets at 28%; the 3-year cap prices all three at 26%; and so on. Every caplet below is a Black calculation exactly like Chapter 1 — same $F=K=3\%$, just different $\sigma$ and $T=t_i$.

Step B — peel off each new layer

The new caplet introduced at maturity $n$ is the difference between consecutive cap prices. Everything the two caps share cancels:

PEEL $$ \text{new layer}_n = \text{Cap}(T_n)-\text{Cap}(T_{n-1}) $$
arithmetic · subtract consecutive cap prices layer 1 = 0.003577 - 0 = 0.003577 layer 2 = 0.008049 - 0.003577 = 0.004472 layer 3 = 0.012824 - 0.008049 = 0.004775 layer 4 = 0.017898 - 0.012824 = 0.005075 layer 5 = 0.023395 - 0.017898 = 0.005496
▲ The subtle bit nobody states out loud

Layer 2's price (0.004472) is what the year-2 caplet is worth after the peel. But in Step A, the year-2 caplet inside the 2y cap was priced at 28% and came to 0.004708. Those disagree — on purpose. The peel mixes a 28%-priced cap with a 30%-priced cap, so the leftover (0.004472) corresponds to neither quote. Step C asks: what single vol makes a standalone year-2 caplet worth exactly 0.004472? That answer is the stripped forward vol.

Step C — solve for the stripped vol (one unknown)

We have the layer's price and we want its vol. Black's formula can't be turned inside-out algebraically, so we invert it numerically by bisection: guess a vol, price the caplet, see if it's too high or too low, halve the gap, repeat. Here's the actual hunt for layer 2's vol (target price 0.004472, single caplet at $t=2$):

Skip #3

SABR at one node, plugged into the formula

The main guide says SABR "returns, for instance, $\sigma(2\%)\approx21.5\%$" and moves on. Where? SABR doesn't simulate anything live — it uses Hagan's closed-form approximation, an explicit (if intimidating) formula. We'll write it out, name every piece, then grind the three example strikes through it.

HAGAN '02 (lognormal) $$ \sigma(K)=\frac{\alpha}{(FK)^{\frac{1-\beta}{2}}\Big[1+\frac{(1-\beta)^2}{24}\ell^2+\frac{(1-\beta)^4}{1920}\ell^4\Big]}\cdot\frac{z}{x(z)}\cdot\big[1+\Lambda\,T\big] $$ $$ \ell=\ln\tfrac{F}{K},\quad z=\frac{\nu}{\alpha}(FK)^{\frac{1-\beta}{2}}\ell,\quad x(z)=\ln\!\frac{\sqrt{1-2\rho z+z^2}+z-\rho}{1-\rho} $$ $$ \Lambda=\frac{(1-\beta)^2}{24}\frac{\alpha^2}{(FK)^{1-\beta}}+\frac{\rho\beta\nu\alpha}{4(FK)^{\frac{1-\beta}{2}}}+\frac{2-3\rho^2}{24}\nu^2 $$
The pieces, in plain words
  • The big fraction $\alpha/(FK)^{\dots}[\dots]$ is the ATM backbone — the vol level right at the money.
  • $z/x(z)$ is the skew/smile bender. At the money $\ell=0\Rightarrow z=0$ and $z/x(z)\to1$ (no bend). Away from the money it lifts the wings.
  • $[1+\Lambda T]$ is a small time correction — here it's about $+8.5\%$ and nearly constant across strikes.
  • Our node: $F=3\%,\ \alpha=0.027,\ \beta=0.5,\ \rho=0,\ \nu=0.45,\ T=5$. With $\rho=0$ the middle term of $\Lambda$ drops out.

Strike-by-strike, every intermediate shown

$x(z)$ looks scary but it's just "the curvy correction"; when $z=0$ it equals 1. Watch all four ingredients ($\ell$, $z$, $x(z)$, backbone denominator, $\Lambda T$) for each strike:

◈ Why the smile is a smile

Look at the $z/x(z)$ column: it's 0.92 at $K{=}2\%$, exactly 1.00 at the money, 0.78 at $K{=}5\%$ — but the backbone denominator grows with $K$. The two effects fight, and the result is high–low–high across strikes: 21.5% → 16.9% → 19.3%. That up-down-up is the grin. Four parameters, and the whole curve falls out of one formula evaluated at each strike.

The bilinear blend: where the corner vols come from

This is the worst offender. The main guide's worked example lists "corner σ at matched K" as 29.67%, 29.30%, 27.30%, 26.94% — as if read off a shelf. They are not. Each is a fresh SABR evaluation at a strike that's different for every corner. Here's the entire chain for the query T*=0.7y, τ*=3y, K=3%.

Stage 2 — the interpolated forward and the moneyness (the skipped middle)

Before reading any smile, the engine blends the four corner forwards (each from the cube's forward curve $F=0.022+0.0015\ln(1{+}\tau)+0.001\ln(1{+}T)$) into one query forward $F^*$, then measures how far the strike sits from it:

▲ The moneyness twist, made concrete

We do not read every corner's smile at K=3%. We read each at its own strike that sits +54.8bp above its own forward, so all four are compared at equal distance-from-ATM. That's why each corner gets a different matched strike $K_{ij}=F_{ij}+m$ below.

Bonus

Breeden–Litzenberger: the differentiation, in full

The main guide differentiates the call price twice and lands on "$\partial^2C/\partial K^2=\phi(K)$," but the two differentiation steps each hide a calculus move (the Leibniz rule for differentiating an integral whose limit contains the variable). Here are both moves, slowly.

From expected payoff to a probability density
Start: the call is the average payoff over the density $\phi$ of the future rate. Only outcomes above $K$ pay $(x-K)$:
$$ C(K)=\int_K^\infty (x-K)\,\phi(x)\,dx $$
"Sum, over every possible future rate $x$, of how much it pays times how likely it is."
Differentiate in $K$. Leibniz says: when $K$ appears both in the lower limit and inside the integrand, you get a boundary term plus an interior term:
$$ \frac{dC}{dK}=\underbrace{-(x-K)\phi(x)\Big|_{x=K}}_{\text{boundary}}\;+\;\int_K^\infty \frac{\partial}{\partial K}(x-K)\,\phi(x)\,dx $$
The boundary term comes from the moving limit; the interior term from $K$ inside the integrand. Handle each.
The boundary term vanishes: at $x=K$ the factor $(x-K)=0$. The interior derivative is $\partial_K(x-K)=-1$. So:
$$ \frac{dC}{dK}=0+\int_K^\infty(-1)\,\phi(x)\,dx=-\int_K^\infty\phi(x)\,dx=-\Pr(F_T>K) $$
The slope of price-vs-strike is (minus) the probability of finishing in the money. Half-way there.
Differentiate again. Now $K$ sits only in the lower limit of $\int_K^\infty\phi\,dx$. The fundamental theorem of calculus: $\frac{d}{dK}\int_K^\infty\phi=-\phi(K)$. With the leading minus sign:
$$ \frac{d^2C}{dK^2}=-\big(-\phi(K)\big)=\boxed{\;\phi(K)\;} $$
The curvature of price-vs-strike is the probability density. A density can't be negative, so $\partial^2C/\partial K^2\ge0$ everywhere — that's the no-butterfly-arbitrage law.

Believe it numerically

Take a clean lognormal ($F=3\%,\ \sigma=20\%,\ T=2$). Compute $\partial^2C/\partial K^2$ by the finite-difference $\big(C(K{+}h)-2C(K)+C(K{-}h)\big)/h^2$ and compare to the lognormal density formula. They should agree:
